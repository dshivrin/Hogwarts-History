# The Departure of Salazar Slytherin

The Houses could preserve four different judgements of promise while their
founders still agreed upon one school. Courage, learning, diligence and
ambition might compete for honour without deciding whether a magical child
belonged at Hogwarts at all. Salazar Slytherin's ancestry condition crossed
that boundary. It did not ask which pupils he preferred to teach. It sought to
exclude pupils of Muggle parentage from the education the founders had created
together.

The rupture matters because it did not begin with four enemies. The founders
had built the castle together, sought magical children and worked in harmony
for several years. Difference had been present without destroying cooperation.
Only gradually, according to the strongest surviving historical account, did a
rift grow between Slytherin and the others.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004 -->

Slytherin believed that magical learning should remain within all-magical
families. He distrusted children of Muggle parentage and wanted Hogwarts to be
more selective in admitting them. This was more than a preference for
pure-blood pupils within his own House. It was a proposed boundary around the
school. The distinction is confirmed by the outcome: the demand divided the
founders, but it did not become the ordinary rule of Hogwarts.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004 -->

The record names Godric Gryffindor most clearly in opposition. After the
disagreement had continued for some time, Slytherin and Gryffindor had what
Professor Binns described as a serious argument on the subject. We are not
given their words, the place in which they quarrelled or a formal proceeding by
which the matter was judged. Nor are Hufflepuff and Ravenclaw assigned separate
parts in the confrontation. The rift was with the other founders; the argument
that survives by name was with Gryffindor.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004 -->

School tradition remembers a more violent breakdown. The Sorting Hat, which
places itself among the events, tells of discord spreading through the school,
Houses turning upon one another, and a period of duelling and fighting in which
friend opposed friend. A song is not a casualty list or a sequence of dated
incidents. It cannot establish a single decisive duel between Gryffindor and
Slytherin, still less its spells or victor. It does preserve the longstanding
belief that the founders' dispute escaped the bounds of argument and threatened
the young institution around them.

<!-- evidence: ootp-ch11-003 -->

Read together, the accounts give a progression without supplying a scene. The
founders cooperated; disagreements emerged; Slytherin's demand opened a rift;
the quarrel with Gryffindor became serious; and conflict widened before
Slytherin departed. Where the chronology cannot be made finer, it is better to
retain its honest compression. An invented council, vote or final combat would
make the story more vivid only by making it less historical.

Slytherin left Hogwarts. That is the firm conclusion of the earlier history,
and the Hat's tradition agrees that his departure brought the fighting to an
end. Neither account says that he resigned freely, was expelled under a school
law or departed because he lost a duel. The precise degree of choice cannot be
recovered from them.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->

A much later official account describes the other three founders as having
forced Slytherin out. That clarification sharpens the balance of power but does
not recover the missing process. It supplies no vote, sentence or final
encounter, and it belongs to evidence published long after the original
historical narrative. The safest conclusion remains that Slytherin's position
became incompatible with that of his fellow founders and that his departure
ended the immediate conflict without resolving the question that caused it.

Hogwarts did not depart with him. The Hat remembers the four founders reduced
to three and the fighting subsiding rather than the school dissolving. No
surviving account describes the next term, the redistribution of Slytherin's
teaching or the first decisions made by those who remained. Continuation need
not have meant ease. It meant that the institution proved capable of surviving
the loss of one of its makers.

<!-- evidence: ootp-ch11-003 -->

Slytherin House survived as well. This is one of the most consequential choices
visible in the result, though the deliberation behind it is lost. The school
retained Slytherin's name and his place among the four founders even after
rejecting the ancestry restriction for which he had broken with the others.
Retention was not adoption. Hogwarts did not thereby make exclusion its policy,
and later pupils did not become parties to their founder's dispute merely by
being Sorted into his House.

Yet the founder's departure acquired a second ending in Hogwarts tradition.
Reliable history stopped with the quarrel and his leaving; the story of the
Chamber of Secrets began where that history ended. It held that Slytherin had
constructed a concealed chamber within the castle without the knowledge of the
other founders. Before going, he was said to have sealed it so completely that
no one could open it until his true heir arrived at Hogwarts.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-005 -->

Within the hidden room, according to the story, waited a horror that only the
heir could control. Its purpose was not defence of the school but a future
purge: the removal of those whom Slytherin considered unworthy to study magic.
The legend thus transformed an argument about admission into a threat against
pupils already admitted. What Slytherin had failed to make school policy, his
heir would supposedly enforce through fear and violence.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-005 -->

The monster was not identified in the accepted pre-confirmation account, nor
was the means of entry established. Later school lore strongly associated
Slytherin with the ability to speak to snakes, an association reflected in the
serpent emblem of his House. Parseltongue therefore fitted the Chamber story,
but fitness is not proof. For a historian writing before the Chamber's later
discovery, a remembered founder, an unknown monster and a hereditary claim
remained parts of a tradition rather than a surveyed place.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch11-004 -->

Subsequent evidence changed that judgement. The Chamber was physically entered
and observed. A snake-marked mechanism concealed in a bathroom answered to
Parseltongue; deeper controls bore the same restriction; and the central space
was lined with monumental serpent imagery. Most decisively, a Parseltongue
command released an enormous Basilisk from the statue associated with
Slytherin. These discoveries confirmed the legend's core claims: there was a
hidden Chamber, it contained a monster, and its access and control had been
bound to the ability for which Slytherin and his line were remembered.

The later official account goes further. It identifies the Chamber as
Slytherin's secret construction, the Basilisk as the monster he intended for
himself or his descendants to control, and Parselmouth ability as the lock that
excluded the other founders and ordinary staff. A later claimant to the title
of Slytherin's heir also joined descent and Parseltongue in his own account.
That claim is not a neutral genealogy, but the observed mechanisms no longer
leave the Chamber itself dependent upon his word.

Before those discoveries, disbelief was not mere obstinacy. Hogwarts had been
searched repeatedly by learned witches and wizards and by successive
headmasters and headmistresses. No accepted trace of chamber or monster was
found. A detailed tale had survived for centuries, but so had the failure of
capable investigators to verify it. Under those conditions, a careful
historian could preserve the legend while refusing to promote it into fact.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-006 -->

The mistake lay in turning failure to find the Chamber into proof that it did
not exist. The later explanation is both simple and severe: the searchers did
not possess the language required to reveal the entrance. Stone, wall and
plumbing could be examined without answering the one command upon which access
depended. The better the investigators knew the visible castle, the more
confidently the invisible condition defeated them.

The entrance itself had also changed. A later account says that the original
approach used a concealed trapdoor and magical tunnels. When more elaborate
plumbing reached Hogwarts in the eighteenth century, building work threatened
the old access point. Corvinus Gaunt, a descendant of Slytherin then at the
school, secretly preserved it beneath a new bathroom. The pipe and sink found
centuries later were therefore not founder-era conveniences. They were an
adaptation that allowed an older secret to survive a changing castle.

This alteration does not render the earlier searches foolish, nor does it tell
us how each was conducted. It shows instead why absence and concealment must be
kept distinct. Hogwarts preserved a tradition about a place that could not be
entered by ordinary discovery, while alterations to the castle changed the
form of its entrance without exposing its purpose. The legend endured because
it was never wholly forgotten; scepticism endured because memory supplied no
door that investigators could open.

Slytherin's longer shadow was not confined to masonry. The ancestry question
survived because it belonged to wizarding society as well as to one founder.
Later school history records a highly capable Muggle-born pupil subjected to a
blood-status slur even while Hogwarts admitted and rewarded her. Beyond the
school, an influential pure-blood family continued to treat Muggle descent as
inferior. Later still, a captured Ministry converted the prejudice into
registration, suspicion and punishment.

These events do not prove an unbroken organisation descending from Slytherin,
and they do not make his House collectively responsible for them. They show why
his dispute remained intelligible to later generations. The same false measure
could reappear in a pupil's insult, a family's pride or a government's decree,
while Hogwarts itself continued to educate children whom Slytherin would have
excluded. Founder doctrine and school policy had parted when he did.

The Chamber gave that ideological survival a physical counterpart. For
centuries the school kept Slytherin's name in the open and his alleged secret
beneath suspicion. Later evidence showed that the contrast was sharper still:
the House belonged visibly to Hogwarts, while a concealed mechanism waited to
enforce the founder's rejected boundary by violence. The institution had
survived the rupture, but survival had not removed all that the rupture left
behind.

The founding era therefore ends with three founders continuing a school built
by four. They inherited Slytherin's pupils, his House and the consequences of
his departure, while the castle inherited a secret they did not know how to
find. From this point, Hogwarts's history belongs increasingly to that castle:
not a fixed monument to its makers, but a place capable of preserving,
altering and concealing their work long after they were gone.
