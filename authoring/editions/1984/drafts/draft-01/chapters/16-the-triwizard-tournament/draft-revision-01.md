# Chapter 16 — The Triwizard Tournament

The three schools did not merely know of one another. At some point their
relationship acquired a recurring form in which each accepted the presence,
judgement and rivalry of the other two. That institution was the Triwizard
Tournament. It did more than bring pupils from Hogwarts, Beauxbatons and
Durmstrang into the same place. It required three independent schools to
recognise a host, send a representative, submit to common trials and accept a
shared result.

The fullest account dates the Tournament's establishment only approximately,
to some seven centuries before its revival in 1994. An exact year cannot be
recovered from so rounded a statement, nor does the account name founders or
preserve an act of foundation. It does, however, describe the Tournament as a
friendly competition among the three schools and gives it a purpose beyond the
identification of a winner: it was intended to establish ties among young
witches and wizards of different nationalities.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-007 -->
<!-- source-access: Dumbledore's 1994 explanation carries the approximate foundation and stated purpose of the older Tournament. The paragraph preserves the approximation and does not invent a charter, founders or exact date. -->

Later participants expressed the same purpose in the language of international
cooperation and magical understanding. Their words belong to the revival, not
to an unknown medieval settlement, but they clarify the ideal that organisers
believed themselves to be restoring. Friendship was not an alternative to
competition. The Tournament pursued friendship through competition, placing
school loyalty within a form that obliged its participants to meet one another
under rules none could supply alone.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-007, gof-ch23-005, gof-ch37-005 -->

The old arrangement can be stated simply. Hogwarts, Beauxbatons and
Durmstrang each put forward one champion. Those three champions undertook three
magical tasks, and the Tournament was held once every five years, with the
schools taking turns to host it. Whether every detail was present from the
first Tournament is not independently recorded. What survives is the
institution's remembered form: three schools, three representatives, three
trials and a host that changed.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-007 -->

Rotation mattered. A permanent host might have treated the Tournament as an
occasion extended to guests; a changing host made each school alternately the
provider and recipient of hospitality. No participant possessed the whole
institution. Hogwarts could govern its own grounds when the Tournament came
to the castle, but it could not turn a three-school competition into an
ordinary Hogwarts exercise without destroying the equality on which the event
depended.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-007, gof-ch17-002 -->

The champion embodied that equality. The surviving description does not
present three unaffiliated young magicians who happened to compete at the same
time. Each stood for a school. Victory consequently joined individual skill to
institutional honour, and an imbalance in the number of champions threatened
more than personal fairness. When an anomalous second Hogwarts name appeared
in 1994, the objections from Beauxbatons and Durmstrang were framed as an
advantage improperly gained by the host school. The protest reveals how firmly
one champion and one school were joined in the revived institution.

The later revival also shows what hosting could demand, though not what every
earlier host had done. In 1994 Hogwarts shortened lessons and assembled the
school to welcome delegations from Beauxbatons and Durmstrang. The visitors
arrived in a winged-horse carriage and a ship that rose from the lake, and
their pupils entered the Great Hall and sat among the Hogwarts Houses. For
that year, hospitality required the host to alter its timetable and admit two
other school communities into spaces normally organised around its own.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch15-005, gof-ch15-007, gof-ch16-001 -->

Hospitality did not soften rivalry into politeness. A later account describes
relations between Hogwarts and Beauxbatons as generally cordial while making
their Tournament rivalry equally plain. The two qualities were not
contradictory. Cordiality allowed the schools to recognise one another as
peers; competition gave that recognition stakes. The schools could value the
connection and still care intensely which of them won.

The remaining working methods of the older Tournament are poorly
preserved. Three tasks are reported, and the heads of the participating
schools were serving as judges in the securely dated Tournament of 1792. No
complete old judging panel, selection procedure, score scale, prize or rule on
assistance survives. The detailed machinery familiar from the later revival
must therefore be approached as evidence of a reconstruction, not as a window
left continuously open upon the Middle Ages.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-007, gof-ch15-003 -->

When the Tournament returned in 1994, the Goblet of Fire was used to choose
the intended three champions. Candidates submitted slips bearing a name and
school, and the Goblet was described as an impartial selector. Ordinary entry
was therefore voluntary before selection. Selection itself was treated quite
differently: once the Goblet returned a name, the result constituted a binding
magical contract and the champion was required to compete.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch16-002, gof-ch16-003, gof-ch17-003 -->
<!-- source-access: This paragraph is wholly later editorial history describing the 1994 procedure. No claim is made that the Goblet selected champions in the older Tournament or that its origin is known. -->

The distinction between eligibility and obligation proved severe. A newly
agreed rule restricted the revival to pupils aged seventeen or above, and an
Age Line guarded the Goblet against younger entrants. Yet a fourth name still
emerged, attached to a school that was not participating. The officials
concluded that the contract bound the selected pupil despite the failure of
the safeguards and the absence of an ordinary voluntary entry. The episode
does not disclose an ancient rule. It shows how the revival's protections
could fail while its obligation continued to operate.

The revived tasks were spread through the school year and were intended to
test magical prowess, daring, deduction and the ability to cope with danger.
For the first task, the champions were told that its nature must remain secret,
that they would enter armed only with their wands and that teachers were not
to help them. Five judges awarded numerical marks, while completion of the
first task furnished the clue for the second. These were clear rules for 1994.
Their clarity is not evidence of antiquity.

Judgement could require more than watching. In the second task, much of what
happened took place beneath the lake, beyond the spectators' sight. The
merpeople reported the champions' conduct to the judges, allowing acts hidden
underwater to affect the result declared on shore. The arrangement illustrates
the revived Tournament's attempt to make unlike performances commensurable:
human and non-human testimony, numerical marks and cumulative standing were
drawn into one decision.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch16-002, gof-ch20-007, gof-ch26-007 -->

Common procedure did not abolish partiality. The panel combined the three
school heads with two Ministry organisers, yet school allegiance remained
visible in the judging, and one Ministry judge privately offered assistance
to a Hogwarts champion. The formal ban on teacher help likewise existed beside
the practical possibility of advice, leaks and favour. The Tournament could
require individual performance while continuing to excite the loyalties of
the institutions behind each individual.

If the surviving administrative history is thin, the history of danger is
plain. The old Tournament was eventually discontinued because its death toll
mounted. No number accompanies that judgement, and no casualty rate can
responsibly be calculated. The wording nevertheless describes accumulation,
not one piece of extraordinary bad luck. Death had recurred often enough to
overwhelm whatever friendship, honour and magical distinction the Tournament
was thought to produce.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-007 -->
<!-- source-access: The mounting death toll and discontinuation are direct elements of the later historical account. No statistics, decision-maker or final incident is inferred. -->

This danger was not merely an intruder upon the institution. The Tournament
tested daring and the ability to cope with danger; success therefore depended
partly upon entering hazards from which others were excluded. Because a
champion carried school honour, risk and prestige strengthened one another.
The more formidable the trial, the more distinction might attend its mastery,
even though the same quality made injury and death harder to prevent.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-007, gof-ch16-002, gof-ch17-002 -->
<!-- source-access: The reported death toll, revival testing aims and school-representation dispute support a bounded interpretation that danger and institutional prestige reinforced one another. No contemporary theory of acceptable risk is invented. -->

The revival tried to preserve one half of that relationship while restraining
the other. Its organisers claimed to have taken measures ensuring that no
champion would face mortal danger, but they did not promise harmless tasks.
The final task, for example, placed staff around the perimeter with a distress
signal for rescue while dangerous creatures and enchantments remained within.
Protection surrounded the hazard and could answer a call from it; protection
did not remove the hazard, for that would have removed part of what was being
tested.

The clearest surviving incident from the older Tournament occurred in 1792.
It deserves unusual confidence because the account is expressly attributed to
*Hogwarts: A History*. The work is known to have been selective in what it
included, but incompleteness does not contradict the particular event it
records. Here, unusually, the later reader identifies not merely an old story
but a statement preserved in the history itself.

In that year the champions were required to catch a cockatrice. The creature
went on the rampage and injured all three heads of the participating schools,
who were serving as judges. The surviving report does not identify the host,
the champions or a winner. It does not explain how the cockatrice escaped
control, describe the injuries or record what the three schools did
immediately afterward.

The importance of the incident lies less in the creature than in the offices
of the injured. The three heads were not incidental spectators. They embodied
their schools and judged the competition those schools had agreed to share.
In 1792, an institution intended to join Hogwarts, Beauxbatons and Durmstrang
endangered all three people responsible for governing it. The disorder of a
task had reached the authority meant to contain and assess it.

Nothing in the surviving account makes 1792 the final Tournament or attributes
discontinuation to a decree passed in its aftermath. To do so would replace an
unknown sequence with an attractive single cause. The evidence instead joins
abandonment to a mounting death toll. The cockatrice disaster stands as the
best-documented example of the danger that accumulated, not as a complete
explanation of when or how the institution ended.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch15-003, gof-ch12-007 -->

By 1994 the Tournament had not been held for more than a century. Even that
long interval does not yield an exact date for its last earlier occurrence.
Nor does the surviving history identify the authority that stopped it. There
is no recorded final vote of the three schools, ministerial order,
international ruling or solitary disaster that can be made to bear the whole
decision. It is safer to describe the result than to invent its machinery:
accumulated danger made continuation unacceptable, and the Tournament ceased.

Discontinuation did not erase the form that had been abandoned. Several
attempts were made over the intervening centuries to reinstate the Tournament,
none very successfully. No dates, sponsors, venues or proposed safeguards are
given for these efforts, and Hogwarts cannot be placed securely in every
discussion. Their repeated occurrence nevertheless shows that the idea
retained its hold. The three-school competition remained recognisable after it
ceased to be practicable.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-007 -->

This persistence is historically important. A tradition may disappear because
its operation has failed while surviving as a model of what its institutions
once attempted together. The Tournament's long absence was therefore not an
empty interval. It preserved a disagreement that later organisers would have
to confront: whether the old combination of representation, rivalry and danger
could be restored without restoring the death toll that had defeated it.

In 1994 another attempt finally reached the point of operation. Planning had
occupied the British Ministry's Departments of International Magical
Cooperation and Magical Games and Sports during the summer. The heads of the
three schools agreed to the new age restriction, while later objections refer
to the meetings, negotiations and compromises by which the arrangements had
been reached. The revival was thus neither a private Hogwarts decision nor the
automatic resumption of a dormant calendar. It was a negotiated reconstruction
with substantial British governmental organisation.

The reconstruction preserved the features by which the Tournament was still
recognisable: Hogwarts, Beauxbatons and Durmstrang; one intended champion from
each; three tasks; a host school; and honour attached to a shared result. Around
that core it placed arrangements known securely only from the revival—the
seventeen-year threshold, the Age Line, the Goblet's submission procedure, a
five-person judging panel, numerical scoring, Ministry organisers and explicit
rescue precautions. Old form and new protection were deliberately joined, but
they should not be mistaken for a single body of ancient rules.

The selection failure exposed the limits of that design. The Age Line guarded
who could approach the Goblet, not every means by which a name might be placed
inside it. The Goblet could be made to recognise a false fourth-school
category, and the binding contract then compelled officials to honour a result
produced outside their intended procedure. Impartiality within supplied
categories was not immunity from deception. A shared institution could possess
neutral rules and still depend upon the security of the assumptions beneath
them.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch16-002, gof-ch16-003, gof-ch17-001, gof-ch17-003, gof-ch35-002 -->

The safety failure requires an equally careful distinction. Cedric Diggory was
not killed by an intended obstacle or an error of ordinary judging. The altered
Cup removed two Hogwarts champions from the final task, and Cedric was murdered
after their arrival elsewhere. Hostile intervention, not the planned design of
the task, caused his death. Yet the attack succeeded through the Tournament's
machinery: manipulated selection placed its target inside the competition, and
the object marking the task's goal became the means of removal. Reform had
reduced some visible dangers without making the institution proof against
deliberate misuse.

Much later testimony describes the 1994–95 competition as the last Tournament
and says that it was cancelled afterward. It supplies no decree, date,
decision-maker or proof of a permanent legal prohibition. Nor did the
catastrophe destroy the relationship that the Tournament had expressed.
Dumbledore closed the year by affirming that the visitors from Beauxbatons and
Durmstrang would remain welcome at Hogwarts. The competition could fail again
without converting its participants into strangers.

For Hogwarts, the Tournament revealed a form of international participation
more demanding than contact alone. The school accepted a cycle it did not own,
a champion who represented it, hosts it could not command, judges beyond its
sole control and rules that had to be recognised across institutional
boundaries. At Hogwarts itself the castle could receive the other schools and
provide the ground on which the contest operated, but hosting did not confer
the whole authority. The Tournament worked only while three schools consented
to be peers.

Its failure is no less revealing than its creation. The old Tournament joined
friendship to rivalry and magical distinction to danger until mounting deaths
made the combination intolerable. The revival tried to preserve prestige while
separating danger from mortal danger, but rules, age restrictions and rescue
arrangements could not make that boundary secure against every failure. The
Tournament was easier to remember than to operate safely, and easier to revive
in form than to free from the tension that had ended it.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-007, gof-ch16-002, gof-ch31-003, gof-ch31-006, gof-ch32-002, gof-ch35-002, cc-p1-a1-s10-002 -->

A school devoted to magic could restrict entry, devise protections, supervise
hazards and abandon a tradition when its cost became too great. What it could
not do was remove danger from magic altogether. The Triwizard Tournament made
that limit unusually visible because danger was not merely what the institution
feared. It was also part of what the institution asked its champions to face.
