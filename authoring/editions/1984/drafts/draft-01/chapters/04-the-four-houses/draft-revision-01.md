# The Four Houses

Hogwarts was founded as one school, but it was never intended to make all its pupils alike. The four who established it agreed upon the need to teach young witches and wizards in safety, and upon the value of bringing them together. They did not, however, agree that the same qualities deserved the greatest encouragement. Their solution was not to divide the school itself. It was to preserve four judgements within it.

The names of Gryffindor, Hufflepuff, Ravenclaw and Slytherin therefore came to signify more than the founders. They became the names of the communities into which pupils were received. Professor Binns, in his account of the school’s beginnings, describes the founders as building the castle together and each seeking pupils to instruct; the Sorting Hat’s own traditional songs remember those pupils as divided according to the founders’ differing preferences. <!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

No surviving school charter tells us that the Houses were planned in their finished form on a single day. Indeed, the most detailed account belongs to song and ceremony rather than to administrative record. Yet the tradition is consistent on the essential point: the founders’ differences were not merely personal. They were given a lasting place in the institution. <!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

This arrangement may reasonably be understood as a practical compromise. One school could serve the whole magical community while allowing four different ideals to guide the education of its children. That interpretation must remain an interpretation, for the sources do not preserve the founders’ deliberations. What they do preserve is the result: unity without uniformity. <!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

At first, selection appears to have belonged to the founders themselves. Binns says that each sought out young people who showed signs of magic and brought them to the school. The Hat’s songs describe a further act of choice within Hogwarts: each founder favoured particular pupils, and the four divided those they wished to teach. <!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

A distinction is required, however, between finding magical children and assigning them to Houses. The evidence establishes that the founders recruited pupils and that House tradition associates each of them with particular preferences. It does not show that House worthiness determined whether every magical child could receive an education. <!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Nor can we reconstruct the first selections as though we possessed their register. The songs speak confidently of favourites and qualities, but not of interviews, examinations or a fixed ceremony. The earliest pupils may have been chosen by direct acquaintance, by observation, or simply by the founders teaching those whom they had brought to the castle. On such particulars the record is silent.

The silence matters. Later custom makes the Houses appear inevitable, as though every first-year had always sat beneath the Hat while the whole school watched. The first arrangements were almost certainly more personal. The institution came first into the hands of four living teachers; only afterwards did their judgements become a system capable of surviving them. <!-- evidence: ext-a02-001, ext-a02-002, gof-ch12-002 -->

The familiar summaries of the Houses are easily repeated and easily misunderstood. Gryffindor valued courage; Ravenclaw, learning; Hufflepuff, loyalty and hard work; Slytherin, ambition and resourcefulness. These were preferences, not complete descriptions of four kinds of human being. No child arrives at school possessing only one virtue, and no founder’s ideal exhausted the character of every pupil admitted under that founder’s name. <!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003, ootp-ch19-002, ext-a20-004, ext-a20-005 -->

For Gryffindor, bravery meant more than a taste for danger. As a principle of selection it honoured the pupil who would act despite fear, resist pressure, or accept a difficult duty. Yet courage may be rash or patient, conspicuous or almost private. The House could therefore contain pupils whose conduct differed greatly while their decisive choices answered the same ideal.

Ravenclaw’s preference for intelligence and learning was equally broad. Quickness, curiosity, originality and disciplined study do not always appear together. A pupil might be learned without being imaginative, or imaginative without being orderly. Ravenclaw’s standard directed attention towards the life of the mind; it did not reduce that life to examination marks or a single measure of cleverness. <!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Hufflepuff is most often wronged by the old story. The tradition that Helga Hufflepuff was willing to teach those whom the others did not choose has sometimes been treated as proof that her House had no standard at all. The same tradition expressly praises justice, loyalty, patience and labour. Her welcome was not an absence of values. It was a conviction that diligence and fair dealing were worthy of honour, and that education ought not to be reserved only for the conspicuously bold, brilliant or well-born. <!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Slytherin admired ambition, determination, cunning and the ability to preserve oneself in difficulty. These too are qualities rather than verdicts upon character. Ambition may serve a generous purpose or a selfish one; resourcefulness may rescue others or merely advance its possessor. The later reputation of Slytherin House must not be allowed to turn every quality valued by its founder into a vice. <!-- evidence: ext-a20-004, ext-a20-005, ext-a02-003 -->

The later history of Sorting confirms this overlap. Pupils have been difficult to place between Gryffindor and Ravenclaw, and between Gryffindor and Slytherin. Such uncertainty is not a failure of the system. It reveals that the Houses deal in competing emphases within complex persons. The Hat decides where a pupil may belong; it does not discover a simple species to which that pupil has always belonged. <!-- evidence: ootp-ch19-002, ext-a20-001 -->

The founders eventually faced a difficulty which no school can avoid: an arrangement dependent upon four persons cannot survive those persons unchanged. The accepted tradition holds that Godric Gryffindor supplied his own hat and that all four founders placed enough of their judgement within it to continue the work of selection. The object was therefore not merely a memorial. It was a means of succession. <!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003, ext-a02-001, ext-a02-002 -->

The Sorting Hat examines the pupil rather than receiving a spoken claim. It can perceive thoughts and qualities which may not be obvious to the watching school. It can also hesitate. The rare prolonged decisions known as Hatstalls show that some pupils answer strongly to more than one House, while other accounts show the Hat responding to a pupil’s own wishes. <!-- evidence: ext-a02-002, ext-a20-001, ext-a20-003 -->

<!-- source-access: ext-a02-002, ext-a20-001 and ext-a20-003 are later official editorial sources describing the Hat's operation and close decisions. -->

Preference, however, is not command. A frightened child may ask for a House which seems safer, grander or more familiar; the Hat remains charged with judgement. One later recollection of Harry Potter’s Sorting records his urgent wish not to enter Slytherin, a wish the Hat considered together with the qualities it had found. Another account tells of Neville Longbottom asking for Hufflepuff while the Hat judged him suited to Gryffindor. In both cases the child’s desire mattered, but it did not replace the Hat’s task. <!-- evidence: cc-p1-a1-s02-003, ext-a20-003, ext-a02-002 -->

Thus the ceremony joins inheritance to individual judgement. The Hat speaks with the founders’ remembered voices, yet it addresses the particular child before it. Its decision preserves the old distinctions without pretending that living pupils are identical to one another—or to pupils selected a thousand years before.

In the mature school, Sorting is brief, but its consequences shape the whole school career. The first visible effect is a place at a House table among older pupils who applaud the newcomer’s arrival. From that evening, the House provides companions, a common room and dormitory, and a first circle of belonging within the much larger castle. <!-- evidence: ps-ch07-002, gof-ch12-003 -->

The arrangement is social, residential and pastoral at once. A Head of House holds particular responsibility for the pupils assigned to it. Members share lessons with their own House and, for some subjects, with another. They therefore live inside a smaller community without being educated in complete isolation from the rest of the school. <!-- evidence: gof-ch12-003, poa-ch07-001 -->

House membership also makes individual conduct public. Merits and offences are translated into points; a pupil’s action can raise or diminish a collective total. The hourglasses that display those totals give daily behaviour a visible consequence, while the House Cup turns the year’s accumulation into honour shared by pupils who may scarcely know one another. <!-- evidence: cos-ch05-005, ps-ch15-002, ps-ch17-005 -->

This system teaches several lessons at once, not all of them comfortable. It rewards service to companions and gives older and younger pupils a common interest. It also ensures that a mistake may be resented by people who did not make it. A House can offer protection and friendship, but it can also become an audience before which every triumph or humiliation is enlarged.

By the later history of Hogwarts, a new pupil could be told that a House would be something like a family. The comparison is useful if it is not made sentimental. Families give identity, memory and mutual obligation; they also transmit expectations and quarrels. The Houses do the same. <!-- evidence: gof-ch12-003, ps-ch06-005, hbp-ch04-005, dh-ch33-005 -->

The founders’ ideals could not remain unchanged once generations of pupils began to inherit them. Children arrived already knowing the House names. Some knew where their parents, brothers or sisters had belonged; some treated that history as a promise, others as a burden. The Black family expected Slytherin, yet Sirius Black entered Gryffindor. Several Weasleys shared one House, but such patterns were customs of family and character, not a law of blood. <!-- evidence: ps-ch06-005, hbp-ch04-005, dh-ch33-005 -->

The distinction is important. A House may run through a family because stories, loyalties and admired qualities run through it. That does not make Sorting hereditary. The Hat judges the child, and the child may depart sharply from expectation.

Competition strengthened these identities. Points were counted in public, the House Cup awarded at the year’s end, and contests between Houses carried the pride of whole communities. Even decoration could make victory appear to transform the Great Hall itself. Such customs gave pupils reasons to cooperate with their housemates, but also reasons to interpret ordinary school life as a contest among four camps. <!-- evidence: ps-ch17-005, poa-ch12-004 -->

In later years, rivalry sometimes exceeded friendly competition. House colours, badges and songs could become instruments of mockery, and old reputations could be applied to a pupil before that pupil had done anything to deserve them. This development does not tell us when hostility began, and the evidence supplies no single moment at which House pride became faction. It does show how an institutional identity, repeated daily and rewarded collectively, can harden into prejudice. <!-- evidence: ootp-ch19-005 -->

The Houses therefore preserved more than virtues. They preserved memories of comparison: brave against clever, steadfast against ambitious, one table against another, one family tradition against its neighbour’s. The founders had made difference governable. Their successors discovered that governable difference could still become antagonism.

One of the four preferences was not like the others. Courage, intelligence, loyalty and ambition describe qualities which any child might display. Salazar Slytherin’s distrust of pupils born to Muggle families imposed a condition of ancestry. It did not ask what a pupil might become. It questioned whether that pupil ought to be taught at Hogwarts at all. <!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

For a time, the House arrangement could contain disagreements about which virtues deserved the highest honour. It could not so easily contain a dispute about the boundaries of the school community. Hufflepuff’s broad welcome and Slytherin’s restriction were not merely different tastes in pupils; they pointed towards incompatible answers to the question of whom Hogwarts existed to serve. <!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Here the compromise of the Houses reached its limit. The institution could preserve four judgements while all four accepted a common school. Once one founder made ancestry a condition of belonging, the disagreement passed beyond education into exclusion. The old unity had not yet vanished, but its deepest fault had been laid bare. <!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->
