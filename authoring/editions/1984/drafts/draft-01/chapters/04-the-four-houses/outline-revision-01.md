## Chapter argument

Hogwarts preserved difference rather than eliminating it. Tradition remembers
the four founders assigning pupils according to distinct judgements of promise
and placing enough of those judgements in the Sorting Hat for the choices to
continue after them. That tradition supports, but does not independently prove,
the interpretation that Houses first allowed four educational ideals to coexist
inside one school. Over time, assignment became far more than a label: it
created communities of residence, belonging, staff responsibility, collective
reward, expectation, and competition. The same durability that preserved
founder ideals also simplified them into reputations and gave disagreement an
institutional life. Slytherin's ancestry condition finally exposes the limit of
the arrangement, because it concerns entitlement to education rather than one
quality to value within it.

## Opening

Begin from Chapter 3's final fact: Hogwarts had become one continuing school,
but its founders still judged promise differently. Pose the institutional
problem without repeating the biographies or the foundation: how could one
school preserve four standards without demanding that one govern everyone?
Introduce the Houses as the remembered answer, while identifying the Hat's
account as school tradition rather than a surviving charter.

## Historical Progression

One school with four judgements → founders remembered as selecting preferred
pupils → four overlapping ideals acquire House form → the Hat carries selection
beyond the founders → House assignment develops into daily community and
collective consequence → family expectation and competition strengthen identity
→ Slytherin's ancestry condition reveals the limit of coexistence.

The progression is conceptual and institutional where exact dates are missing.
The outline must not imply a continuous dated sequence for common rooms, points,
Cup, Heads of House, ghosts, or rivalry.

## Treatment of Uncertainty and Tradition

Attribute the Hat's origin and founder-era selection account once near their
first use, then vary natural attribution without repeating disclaimers. State
once that mature House practices are documented later and undated in origin.
Treat difficult Sortings as evidence of overlapping fit, not proof of a known
formula. Keep choice as influence rather than command. Reject hereditary
Sorting explicitly only where family expectation is discussed. Keep the weak
later claim about changing Slytherin composition out of the outline and prose.

## Conclusion

Conclude that the Houses succeeded in preserving the founders' different
judgements, but success brought a cost: institutional identities outlived the
people and could harden into expectation, competition, and inherited
reputation. End on the precise fault line created when Slytherin made ancestry
a condition of access. Do not narrate the quarrel's escalation or outcome; let
the unresolved pressure carry directly into Chapter 5.
