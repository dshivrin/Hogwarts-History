# Chapter 20 — From Dippet to Dumbledore

The succession from Armando Dippet to Albus Dumbledore is preserved without
its handover. Dippet appears securely as Headmaster, Dumbledore appears for
years within his staff, and Dumbledore later occupies the office; no surviving
account joins those points with a date, appointment or ceremony. A less
cautious history might supply the missing scene from probability. Hogwarts has
left enough improbable history to make that a particularly unsafe method.

What can be followed is more revealing than an invented transfer. Teachers and
rules outlasted Dippet; people whom Dumbledore encountered under one Headmaster
remained consequential under the next; decisions made in moments of danger
left places, restrictions and grievances for a successor to inherit. The two
men should therefore be considered not as separate proprietors of two
Hogwartses, but as office-holders within one school whose continuity made their
different judgements matter.

## Dumbledore Inside Dippet's Hogwarts

The earliest useful glimpse is not a great contest of policy, but a school
entertainment gone disastrously wrong. During Dippet's headship, Professor
Herbert Beery attempted a Christmas pantomime of *The Fountain of Fair
Fortune*. Dumbledore was then a young Transfiguration teacher and contributed
stage effects to the production. The episode cannot be securely dated, but it
places the later Headmaster plainly inside Dippet's Hogwarts, doing the work of
a member of staff rather than waiting outside history for his own tenure to
begin.

<!-- source-access: Later official retrospective preserves the undated Dippet-era production and identifies Dumbledore as a young Transfiguration teacher. Chapter 10 owns the pantomime as pupil and Christmas experience; only staff placement is developed here. -->

The production also left less theatrical residues. Dippet placed Silvanus
Kettleburn on probation and forbade further pantomimes at Hogwarts. Kettleburn,
who accumulated sixty-two periods of probation during his career, nevertheless
remained on the staff into Dumbledore's headship; Dumbledore later considered
him much mellowed. Here continuity can be seen in two different forms. A ban
imposed by one Headmaster endured, and a teacher whom he had judged reckless
continued long enough to be judged again by another.

<!-- source-access: Later official retrospective establishes Dippet's probation and ban, Kettleburn's accumulated probations and Dumbledore's later assessment. No general staffing policy is inferred from this exceptional career. -->

By 1938 Dumbledore's place in the school had acquired a more consequential
function. He went as Hogwarts's representative to the London orphanage in
which Tom Riddle lived, explained that Riddle was a wizard and demonstrated
that magic was real. The encounter belongs here not as the opening of a
biography of Voldemort, but because it was an act performed by a teacher under Dippet.
Dumbledore's long institutional relationship with Riddle began before either
man held the later position by which he would be chiefly remembered.

<!-- evidence: hbp-ch13-003, hbp-ch13-004 -->
<!-- source-access: The 1938 encounter is preserved in a later-carried memory. Chapter 19 owns the visit as evidence of outward institutional reach; here it establishes Dumbledore's school role and the beginning of a relationship that crosses the succession. -->

Riddle's conduct at school sharpened the importance of that beginning. Most of
the staff, Dumbledore later recalled, found the talented and outwardly
well-behaved pupil impressive. Dumbledore did not share their confidence and
kept watch, though his suspicion did not amount to knowledge of every hidden
act or future crime. This difference among teachers would matter in 1943. It
also prevents hindsight from being distributed too generously: Dumbledore had
grounds for distrust, not a complete account of what Riddle had concealed.

## 1943

In 1943 Hogwarts faced attacks which culminated in the death of a pupil. Dippet
was governing a school in danger and under pressure from beyond its walls; he
said that the Ministry was discussing closure. The stakes were therefore not
confined to discipline or investigation. The continued operation of Hogwarts
was in question, while the identity of the attacker remained concealed behind
an account supplied by the person responsible.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch13-005, cos-ch13-007, cos-ch17-003 -->

Riddle asked to remain at Hogwarts during the summer rather than return to the
orphanage. Dippet refused. He told the boy that special arrangements might
have been possible in ordinary circumstances, but not while the attacks had
placed the school under threat of closure. That decision is sometimes easy to
read through knowledge acquired afterwards. Within the circumstances Dippet
described, it was a refusal to keep a pupil in a school whose safety and future
were then uncertain, not evidence that he knew the hidden cause and ignored it.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch13-007 -->
<!-- source-access: The exchange is preserved in the diary memory. It establishes Dippet's stated reason and the closure context, not the full legal position or facts then available to him. -->

The crisis soon appeared to have found its culprit. Riddle accused Rubeus
Hagrid; Hagrid was expelled and his wand was broken. Riddle's later confession
revealed that he had framed Hagrid to close an inquiry which threatened his own
place at Hogwarts. The correction is
decisive about Hagrid's innocence of the alleged offence, but it does not
restore the missing disciplinary record. Who ordered each sanction, by what
procedure, and upon what complete body of evidence cannot now be recovered.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch13-005, cos-ch17-003, ps-ch04-007 -->
<!-- source-access: Riddle's diary account is deceptive; his later confession corrects the accusation. Hagrid's later recollection corroborates expulsion, wand-breaking and retention at Hogwarts but does not supply the missing procedure. -->

Dumbledore's suspicion nevertheless affected what followed. He persuaded
Dippet to allow Hagrid to remain at Hogwarts and to be trained as gamekeeper.
The division of action is important. Dumbledore doubted the accepted story and
advocated for the expelled pupil; Dippet, as Headmaster, authorised the
arrangement which preserved Hagrid's place at the school. Influence and office
met in the same result, and neither should be mistaken for the other.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch17-003, ps-ch04-007 -->
<!-- source-access: Riddle's later confession attributes suspicion and persuasion to Dumbledore and the formal permission to Dippet; Hagrid's later testimony compresses the outcome around Dumbledore's aid. The more precise division is retained. -->

The crisis thus passed into Hogwarts's future without being truly resolved.
Riddle's guilt remained hidden, Hagrid remained within the institution but no
longer as a pupil, and Dumbledore carried forward a suspicion which the formal
settlement had not answered. Dippet's decision had preserved a life at the
school even as the false accusation fixed its limits. This is a mixed
inheritance, not a convenient verdict upon the Headmaster who acted before the
later confession existed.

## Between Two Headmasters

Riddle approached Dippet once more after leaving school with high honours. He
wanted to return as a teacher, particularly in Defence Against the Dark Arts.
Dippet refused because he considered him too young, though he did not exclude
a later application. The encounter supplies no exact year beyond its place
after Riddle's school career. Its historical value is firmer in another
respect: Dippet still held the headship when the first request was made.

In 1945 Dumbledore defeated Gellert Grindelwald. Later public accounts treated
the victory as a defining achievement, and it greatly enlarged a reputation
which would eventually adhere to the Headmaster of Hogwarts. The date is
secure; its relation to the succession is not. Nothing selected for this
history establishes that Dumbledore was already Headmaster in 1945, that the
victory procured the office, or that Dippet departed because of it.

Between Riddle's first application and his later return lies the succession
itself. No date survives, nor any account of Dippet's departure, Dumbledore's
appointment or the authority by which the change was made. The interval must
remain open.

<!-- evidence: hbp-ch20-003, hbp-ch20-007, ps-ch06-003, dh-ch02-005 -->
<!-- source-access: The two teaching requests place different heads in office, while the 1945 evidence supplies no succession fact. The absence of a date or mechanism is preserved rather than resolved by inference. -->

When Riddle returned, he addressed Dumbledore as Headmaster. He was no longer
the newly graduated applicant whom Dippet had refused for youth, and Dumbledore
did not receive him as one. The teacher who had first met Riddle in 1938, had
watched him at school and had suspected him in 1943 now possessed the authority
to deny him institutional access. The second refusal therefore closes the
chronological bracket: the office had changed hands, although the change itself
remains undocumented.

## Dumbledore's Hogwarts: Admission, Sanctuary and Refusal

Dumbledore later observed that, from the time he refused Voldemort's request,
Hogwarts had been unable to keep a Defence Against the Dark Arts teacher for
longer than a year. The association gave one decision an enduring place in the
school's staffing history, though it did not reveal the means by which the
pattern operated. The refusal was Dumbledore's; the yearly instability became
Hogwarts's. That consequence is sufficient here without turning the episode
into a catalogue of teachers or a theory of a curse.

Refusal was only one form taken by Dumbledore's judgement. When Remus Lupin
approached school age, his lycanthropy seemed to make attendance impossible.
After Dumbledore became Headmaster, he admitted the boy and arranged a place in
which the monthly transformation could occur away from other pupils. The
decision altered access for one child through exceptional precautions. It did
not declare that every comparable risk had been solved, still less establish a
general entitlement independent of the Headmaster's judgement.

The precautions did not remain unbreached. Another pupil learnt enough of the
secret to approach Lupin during a transformation and came close to death before
being rescued. Dumbledore required silence about what had happened, and the
accommodation continued. The episode places a necessary limit upon admiration
of the original decision. It shows Dumbledore sustaining an individual
arrangement after serious danger had become plain; it does not show danger
eliminated or a school-wide system reformed.

Sybill Trelawney presented a different case. Dumbledore later said that he had
approached her interview doubtful that Divination should continue at Hogwarts.
She made a prophecy during the meeting, and he changed his decision. He
appointed Trelawney and kept her within the school, where she received both
employment and protection from those who might exploit what she had revealed.
The precise date and formal terms are not preserved, but the choice belonged
before Voldemort's fall and joined appointment to sanctuary in a way the Lupin
case had not.

<!-- evidence: hbp-ch25-004, ext-a33-001 -->
<!-- source-access: Dumbledore's later testimony preserves his initial doubt and the interview's effect; later official retrospective describes protection and the possibility of further prophecy. The prophecy's content, the eavesdropper and the exact appointment date are not developed here. -->

Hagrid, Lupin and Trelawney did not enter or remain at Hogwarts by the same
route. Hagrid stayed through Dippet's formal permission after Dumbledore's
advocacy; Lupin was admitted by Dumbledore under exceptional precautions;
Trelawney was appointed and sheltered within the staff. Their differences are
as important as their resemblance. Together they show a recurring willingness
in Dumbledore to preserve a particular person's place within the institution.
They do not show a written policy, a universal qualification or a steady
movement towards inclusion.

## A Headmaster in Wartime

The reputation Dumbledore brought to the headship had been formed partly
outside it. His defeat of Grindelwald in 1945 became the public measure of
extraordinary magical ability and later stood prominently in accounts of his
life. By the time war again overtook wizarding Britain, the person occupying
the Hogwarts headship was therefore more than an administrator of an old
school. His name had acquired a public force which could alter how enemies and
allies regarded the institution he led.

<!-- evidence: ps-ch06-003, dh-ch02-005 -->
<!-- source-access: Later public and admiring retrospective sources establish the 1945 victory's place in Dumbledore's reputation. They do not date his accession or make the achievement a formal headmasterial power. -->

Later participants linked that reputation directly to Hogwarts during
Voldemort's first rise. McGonagall remembered Dumbledore as the one wizard whom
Voldemort feared. Hagrid independently described Hogwarts as one of the few
safe places and explained its standing in the same personal terms. These are
recollections, not surviving wartime regulations, but their agreement on the
central relationship is significant: the fear attached to Dumbledore affected
the reputation of the school he governed.

<!-- evidence: ps-ch01-002, ps-ch04-005 -->
<!-- source-access: McGonagall and Hagrid provide later participant testimony. McGonagall corroborates Voldemort's fear of Dumbledore; Hagrid supplies the school's remembered comparative safety. Neither documents specific wartime measures. -->

Personal deterrence is not a defensive plan. The surviving accounts name no
new fortification, evacuation arrangement, emergency curriculum or attack upon
the castle during the First Wizarding War. Nor does comparative safety prove
that ordinary school life continued untouched. What can be said is narrower
and still important: the character and reputation of the incumbent changed
Hogwarts's standing in a frightened society, even where the institutional
means behind that standing cannot be reconstructed.

## What One Headship Leaves Another

Across the two administrations, inheritance took several forms. Dippet left a
ban on pantomimes and retained a teacher whom he repeatedly disciplined. His
permission left Hagrid within Hogwarts after expulsion. Under Dumbledore, an
exceptional pupil arrangement and a protective appointment placed Lupin and
Trelawney within the school, while the refusal of Voldemort preceded enduring
instability in a teaching post. These consequences do not point in one moral or
administrative direction. Some preserved people, some preserved restrictions,
and one preserved a problem.

Dippet and Dumbledore both made consequential decisions under conditions they
could not wholly control. The surviving record makes Dumbledore's personal
pattern more visible, partly because it preserves more of his later testimony
and later fame. That unequal survival is not a sound basis for arranging the
men as failure and improvement. Dippet's authority and Dumbledore's influence
met in 1943; Dumbledore later inherited both the office and the unresolved
history within which he had already served.

By 1984 Hogwarts therefore carried decisions made by both men, though not a
complete record of how one yielded the office to the other. Its continuity was
not changelessness. The Headmaster's judgement could preserve a person, close
a door, accept a risk or lend the school a reputation larger than its walls.
Yet each such act entered an institution already inhabited by earlier choices.
Dumbledore changed Hogwarts as its Headmaster; he did not begin with a school
from which Dippet had disappeared without remainder.
