# The Four Founders

From the scattered magical world that preceded Hogwarts, four names emerge
with unusual clarity: Godric Gryffindor, Helga Hufflepuff, Rowena Ravenclaw and
Salazar Slytherin. They were counted among the greatest witches and wizards of
their age. That collective distinction gives them stature but not four complete
lives. The surviving record is much firmer about what they undertook together
than about their childhoods, careers or private motives.

Two voices chiefly shape their early history. Professor Binns preserves the
spare framework of a common school, several years of harmony and a dispute
that eventually divided its makers. The Sorting Hat preserves something less
documentary and more intimate: a continuing school tradition about their
qualities, friendships and hopes. Its songs change with the year in which they
are sung. They may carry an old institutional memory, but their surviving
verses are not transcripts of conversations held at the foundation.

This difference in authority need not make the Hat useless. Where it agrees
with the historical account—that four founders shared the work of educating
magical children—it strengthens the outline already known. Where it supplies
friendship, poetic origins or judgements of character, it preserves tradition
and should be read as such. History is not improved by forcing both voices to
say precisely the same thing.

The four founders did more than receive pupils who happened to find them. They
sought children who showed signs of magic, brought them to one place and
educated them. They worked in harmony for several years. Whatever private
reasons led each to the undertaking, they agreed upon a practical purpose:
young magical people were to be found and taught within a common institution.

The Hat remembers all four as good friends and gives particular shape to that
friendship by pairing Gryffindor with Slytherin and Hufflepuff with Ravenclaw.
Binns confirms cooperation and harmony, not private intimacy. The pairings are
therefore best understood as the school's long memory of its founders rather
than proof of every personal bond. Even so, the tradition matters. Hogwarts
remembered its beginning not as an alliance of strangers but as a shared work
whose later divisions were painful because cooperation had once been real.

No reviewed account explains how the founders met, who first proposed the
school or how they apportioned their labour. Silence on these matters denies us
a scene, not the undertaking itself. Their agreement is visible in its result
and in the years for which they maintained it. To invent the missing
conversation would add colour only by replacing evidence with confidence.

The personal record begins unevenly. Godric Gryffindor alone has a birthplace
that can be named with confidence: the public history identifies Godric's
Hollow. The Hat's older-sounding geography places Gryffindor by a wild moor,
Hufflepuff in a broad valley, Ravenclaw in a glen and Slytherin in a fen. These
landscapes may preserve origin traditions or serve the poetry of contrast.
They do not license a modern map, and friendship among the founders does not
make their places of origin more exact.

What survives more strongly is a set of educational preferences. Gryffindor
valued courage; Hufflepuff, hard work; Ravenclaw, intelligence; and Slytherin,
ambition and cunning. These were not four mutually exclusive kinds of person.
A brave pupil might also be clever, industrious or ambitious. The traditions
show where each founder looked most readily for promise, not that a child could
possess only one worthy quality.

Gryffindor's remembered courage is joined to a little material evidence. A
later official history describes him as an accomplished conventional swordsman
in a period when magical people might defend themselves with steel as well as
with wands. The old goblin-made sword bearing his name accords with that
account. Nothing further is required of it here. Its later appearances, its
connection with the Hat and the competing traditions of ownership belong to
other histories; they do not make Gryffindor a better-documented teacher.

His clearer educational significance appears in opposition. When Slytherin
would have restricted learning at Hogwarts according to ancestry, Gryffindor
became his principal opponent. That action does not disclose a complete
Gryffindor admissions policy, still less prove that he and Hufflepuff held
identical views. It shows him judging the purpose of the institution he had
helped create and rejecting Slytherin's proposed boundary.

Helga Hufflepuff is remembered through two propositions that are sometimes
mistaken for rivals. One song honours hard work as the quality she valued in
pupils; another remembers her as willing to teach broadly and to treat her
pupils alike. A teacher may esteem effort without restricting instruction to
those already exemplary in it. The sources do not explain how Hufflepuff
balanced preference and welcome, but neither statement needs to be discarded
to preserve the other.

Her tradition supplies the broadest positive claim about access among the four
founders. It does not prove that every magical child in Britain was found, that
Hogwarts maintained universal admission thereafter or that all four founders
shared her standard. It establishes the ideal for which Hufflepuff was
remembered: education offered without using a preferred talent as a gate, and
equal treatment within the work she accepted.

Rowena Ravenclaw's surviving educational identity places intellectual ability
at the centre of promise. The Hat remembers her as preferring the cleverest
pupils and the sharpest minds. That preference tells us nothing certain about
the subjects she taught, the books she wrote, the methods she used or her views
on pupils whom another founder selected. It is a criterion of admiration, not
a recovered philosophy in full.

Longstanding school tradition also associates Ravenclaw with a diadem reputed
to enhance wisdom and said to have vanished with her many centuries ago. The
tradition is useful because it shows how later generations condensed her
memory around intellect. It is not a chain of custody or evidence for her
teaching practice, and its later family history need not interrupt this
portrait.

The Hat remembers Ravenclaw and Hufflepuff as especially close. Their
friendship, if the tradition is accepted at its proper level, is more
interesting when it is not made into agreement. One valued intellectual
selection; the other was remembered for teaching broadly. Friendship did not
require the erasure of educational difference, just as difference did not at
first prevent common work.

Salazar Slytherin shared that work for years. He too was counted among the four
great magical people of the age, and the Hat associates him with ambition,
cunning and shrewdness. Those qualities are traditions about what he admired;
they do not explain every motive or supply achievements the record does not
name. His historically consequential position is more specific. He distrusted
pupils of Muggle parentage and wanted magical education at Hogwarts restricted
to children from all-magical families.

Ancestry differed from the other remembered preferences. Courage,
intelligence, hard work and ambition concerned qualities in pupils who might be
taught. Slytherin's condition concerned which magical children were entitled
to enter the undertaking at all. It converted selection among pupils into a
boundary around education itself.

The restriction remained Slytherin's position. Binns describes disagreement
and a serious argument with Gryffindor; he does not describe the ancestry rule
as adopted Hogwarts policy. Later blood-status prejudice cannot be sent
backwards through time as proof that the school endorsed its founder's demand.
Gryffindor's opposition is clear, Hufflepuff's remembered welcome supplies a
further contrast, and Ravenclaw's complete position on admission does not
survive.

For several years, differing preferences had coexisted within the same work.
The evidence does not establish whether all of them were fully formed at the
beginning or only became sharper as the school developed. The Hat's later
account remembers disagreement widening into rivalry and violence; Binns gives
the firmer minimum of disagreements followed by a serious argument. The larger
rupture must be considered in its own place. Here it is enough to recognise
the point at which difference tested the meaning of the founders' agreement.

The founders therefore emerge neither as four complete biographies nor as four
emblems waiting for later pupils to imitate. They were accomplished magical
people joined by the decision to educate children together. Tradition
preserved distinct judgements about promise; history preserved a dispute over
whether ancestry could bar a magical child from that education. Their common
purpose was strong enough to create Hogwarts, but not simple enough to prevent
an argument about whom Hogwarts was for.

The next stage of their history belongs to the institution rather than the
portrait. A purpose, however sincerely shared, is not yet a school. Hogwarts
required a place, continuing work and arrangements capable of surviving the
people who began it. Only then could the founders' judgements acquire the
durable form by which later generations would know them.
