# Chapter Two

## The Four Founders

### Four Names in a Long Memory

Only at the threshold of Hogwarts do its founders emerge from the scattered
world of the preceding chapter. Godric Gryffindor, Helga Hufflepuff, Rowena
Ravenclaw and Salazar Slytherin were counted among the greatest witches and
wizards of their age. Their distinction matters, but so does what they chose
to do with it: they joined their powers in the education of magical children.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Two witnesses provide most of the surviving outline. Professor Binns preserves
the firm public history of their common undertaking, their years of harmony
and the dispute that followed. The Sorting Hat offers a different testimony.
Traditionally associated with all four founders and carrying their remembered
preferences into the life of the school, it speaks as a witness to their
ideals and friendships. Its account is poetic, but its long service at
Hogwarts gives it a place in the history as well as in the ceremony.

<!-- source-access: gof-ch12-002 and ootp-ch11-003 preserve songs recorded after 1984. They are used as later testimony to older Hat tradition; their exact wording is not attributed to the 1984 narrator. -->

The four names are much clearer than the four early lives. *A History of
Magic* identifies Godric's Hollow as Gryffindor's birthplace. For the others,
tradition supplies only the broad landscapes of valley, glen and fen; it also
associates Gryffindor with a wild moor. These may be old traces of origin, but
only Godric's Hollow can be placed with confidence.

<!-- evidence: dh-ch16-003, gof-ch12-002 -->

Their surviving portraits are consequently made from choices rather than
childhoods. Gryffindor is remembered through courage and opposition;
Hufflepuff through labour and an unusually generous conception of teaching;
Ravenclaw through intellectual excellence; Slytherin through ambition,
cunning and a restriction that eventually divided him from his colleagues.
None is a complete life. Together, however, they form a history of four
distinct people attempting one remarkable work.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

### The Work They Chose Together

The founders did more than receive pupils who happened to find them. They
sought out children who showed signs of magic, brought them to one place and
educated them. Four exceptional magical people thus made teaching a deliberate
and continuing enterprise. Their common purpose was practical: children with
magic were to be found, gathered and taught.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004 -->

Binns records that they worked in harmony for several years. The Hat remembers
more: a common hope, friendship among all four, and particular bonds between
Gryffindor and Slytherin and between Hufflepuff and Ravenclaw. Friendship is a
more intimate claim than cooperation, but it suits the substantial fact that
the partnership endured. Hogwarts was not the momentary bargain of four
rivals. It was the sustained work of people able, for years, to combine
different judgements and abilities.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

<!-- source-access: The friendship pairings derive from the Hat testimony recorded in ootp-ch11-003; the revision uses the claimed older tradition without importing the later song's wording into the 1984 narrative. -->

The remembered friendship of Gryffindor and Slytherin is especially
significant. Later history makes their quarrel so prominent that it is easy to
mistake opposition for their original relation. Tradition instead places two
formidable and differently minded wizards within the same friendship and the
same educational project before placing them on opposing sides of its gravest
question. Their disagreement mattered because a genuine partnership already
existed to be damaged.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Hufflepuff and Ravenclaw offer a quieter version of the same achievement.
Tradition pairs two women whose standards of promise were notably different:
one looked to application and an open willingness to teach, the other to
exceptional powers of mind. Their friendship does not prove that they agreed
upon every pupil, but it shows why difference among the founders need not be
read as failure. A common school could be strengthened by more than one answer
to the question of what made a child worth teaching.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

The record does not preserve the founders' first meeting or the division of
their work, and no invented council can improve upon that silence. The choice
of site, the building and concealment of the castle, the means of finding
pupils and the arrangements of the first school belong to the foundation
itself. What matters here is the human fact beneath those later structures:
four accomplished people chose one another as partners.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

### Four Founders, Four Educational Ideals

The founders' remembered preferences concern qualities in pupils rather than
complete theories of education. Gryffindor valued courage; Hufflepuff, hard
work; Ravenclaw, intelligence; Slytherin, ambition and cunning. These ideals
could overlap in any child. They reveal where each founder first looked for
promise, not four mutually exclusive kinds of human nature.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Helga Hufflepuff's distinction lies in what she did when a pupil failed to fit
another founder's preferred measure. The Hat remembers her as willing to teach
the pupils and treat them alike. Hard work remained a quality she honoured,
but it was not merely a gate through which only the already industrious might
pass. Her ideal allowed education itself to disclose worth in children whom a
narrower selection might overlook.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

This tradition gives Hufflepuff a particular place in the partnership without
turning it into proof of universal admission throughout Hogwarts history. It
describes her remembered offer as a teacher. The small golden cup raised in
her common-room portrait adds a modest image to that memory: Helga appears not
alone with a treasure, but addressing her students.

<!-- evidence: ootp-ch11-003, ext-a07-005 -->

Rowena Ravenclaw's ideal was more selective, but no less educational. She is
remembered as seeking the cleverest pupils and the sharpest minds. Such a
standard made intellectual excellence something to be recognised and
cultivated. It does not tell us which subjects she taught or what discoveries
she made; it does show a founder for whom the disciplined powers of the mind
were central to the promise of a pupil.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

The contrast with Hufflepuff is more illuminating than a simple opposition.
One founder's broad willingness to teach could stand beside another's pursuit
of exceptional intellect, and tradition remembers the women as friends. The
lost diadem associated with Ravenclaw, reputed to enhance wisdom, preserved
the same emphasis in school legend long after the object itself had passed
beyond living memory.

<!-- evidence: ootp-ch11-003, dh-ch29-006, dh-ch30-003 -->

Godric Gryffindor enters the record with more physical definition. He had a
named birthplace, was remembered for courage, and was later described as an
accomplished conventional swordsman in an age when a wizard might defend
himself with steel as well as a wand. The old goblin-made sword bearing his
name is therefore more than a House ornament: briefly used here, it recalls a
founder's martial competence beside the courage he prized in pupils.

<!-- evidence: dh-ch16-003, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003, ext-a08-001, ext-a08-003 -->

<!-- source-access: ext-a08-001 and ext-a08-003 are later official retrospective exposition. The revision retains only the founder-era manufacture and swordsmanship claims, with no post-1984 sword history. -->

His courage acquires its greatest historical importance in his opposition to
Slytherin. Tradition remembers the two men first as friends. Binns records
Gryffindor as Slytherin's principal opponent when ancestry became a condition
of magical education. The movement from friendship to argument makes Godric
more than the emblem of a preferred virtue. It shows him applying judgement
to the purpose of the institution he had helped create.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Salazar Slytherin must likewise be seen before the quarrel if the quarrel is to
be understood. He was one of the four great magical people of the age, a
founder who shared the labour of seeking and educating children and remained
in harmony with his colleagues for years. His remembered ambition and cunning
need not be disguised as faults. A school as daring as Hogwarts could hardly
have been made by people without ambition, and cunning may serve invention as
readily as exclusion.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

His ancestry doctrine stands apart from those qualities. Binns records that
Slytherin distrusted pupils of Muggle parentage and wished magical education
restricted to children from all-magical families. This was a judgement about
who should be taught, not merely about the qualities he admired. A
longstanding legend also associated him with a hidden chamber at Hogwarts;
its authenticity remained unverified, but its survival reflects the enduring
reputation left by his division from the other founders.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, cos-ch09-005 -->

### The Limits of Agreement

The founder traditions preserve two related but distinct kinds of selection.
The first concerned the pupils each founder preferred to teach or receive into
a House: courage for Gryffindor, intellect for Ravenclaw, hard work for
Hufflepuff, and ambition, cunning and magical ancestry for Slytherin. These
preferences distinguished one founder's chosen pupils from another's within
the school.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

The second question was wider: should children of Muggle parentage receive
education at Hogwarts at all? Binns places Slytherin's distrust within this
broader dispute. A rift grew between Slytherin and the other founders, and the
most serious argument set him against Gryffindor. The Hat's account of
Slytherin's preferred House therefore helps to describe his selection, while
Binns establishes the larger threat to access to the school.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->

<!-- source-access: ootp-ch11-003 supports the distinction through later-recorded Hat testimony about House preferences; cos-ch09-004 supplies the historically firmer school-wide admissions dispute. -->

This distinction explains why several educational ideals could coexist for
years while the ancestry restriction tested the partnership. Courage,
intelligence, labour, ambition and cunning answer the question, *What promise
does this pupil show?* An ancestry rule asks first, *May this child be taught?*
A child of Muggle parentage might possess every quality the founders admired
and still be excluded before any founder had the opportunity to cultivate it.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

The restriction remained Slytherin's position; the account does not establish
that Hogwarts adopted it as policy. Gryffindor's opposition is clear, and the
rift with the other founders was broader than the final argument between the
two men. Hufflepuff's remembered willingness to teach widely offers a further
contrast. Ravenclaw's complete view of admission does not survive, and need
not be invented to make the disagreement serious.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Nor is it necessary to decide when Slytherin's doctrine first took shape.
Binns records years of harmony before disagreement grew; the Hat preserves
distinct preferences associated with the founders. Together they show a
partnership capable of bearing differences until one difference touched the
right of a magical child to learn. They do not supply the words of the
argument, and none are needed.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->

The later consequences belong elsewhere. Before the rift, four exceptional
people had already made education a common responsibility and sustained that
work together for years. Their courage, labour, intelligence and ambition did
not merely furnish four later House traditions; joined in practice, those
qualities helped create the possibility of Hogwarts.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

The school was not created by their quarrel. It was created by the common
purpose that made the quarrel consequential. The history must now turn from
the founders' characters and ideals to their practical achievement: how did
four remarkable people make a shared educational purpose into a functioning
school?
