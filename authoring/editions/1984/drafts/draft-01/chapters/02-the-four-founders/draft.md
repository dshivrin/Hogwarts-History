# Chapter Two

## The Four Founders

### Four Names in a Long Memory

The four people who answered the need described in the preceding chapter are
among the most famous and least fully known figures in magical history. Godric
Gryffindor, Helga Hufflepuff, Rowena Ravenclaw and Salazar Slytherin have lent
their names to a thousand years of school life. Their own lives have not
survived with anything like the same completeness. We know the undertaking
they shared far better than we know the roads by which they came to it.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Professor Binns's account supplies the firm frame. He names the four founders,
places them more than a thousand years ago and describes them collectively as
the greatest witches and wizards of their age. The praise is considerable,
though not biographical. It does not tell us which was eldest, where three of
them were born, how they were educated, or what any of them did before the
school began.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

One birthplace emerges from a public history. *A History of Magic* identifies
Godric's Hollow as Gryffindor's birthplace. For Hufflepuff, Ravenclaw and
Slytherin, no equally precise origin is established. School tradition places
the four, in broad poetic fashion, amid moor, valley, glen and fen. Such
landscapes may preserve old associations; they cannot responsibly be turned
into three missing birth certificates and a map.

<!-- evidence: dh-ch16-003, gof-ch12-002 -->

Alongside Binns stands a stranger witness. The Sorting Hat is a longstanding
school institution, and its annual song is not fixed from year to year. In
later-recorded songs it claims a memory of the founders and their differing
standards. That is not the same kind of evidence as Binns's historical
summary. An ancient event, a later performance about it and the date at which
the performance was recorded are three different things. The Hat may preserve
old founder tradition without making the wording of a later song available to
an historian in 1984.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, gof-ch12-003, ootp-ch11-003, ext-a02-001 -->

The distinction is useful rather than merely cautious. Binns gives the secure
public outline: four exceptional magical people undertook a common work, kept
it in harmony for some years and then encountered a serious division. The Hat
preserves a more intimate memory of friendship, preferred qualities and
personal pairings. The first account tells us most reliably what occurred; the
second helps us understand how Hogwarts remembered the people who did it.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

This leaves an uneven portrait. Gryffindor has a named birthplace and a famous
sword. Hufflepuff is remembered through an unusually broad educational offer.
Ravenclaw survives chiefly through the reputation of her mind and the legend
of a lost diadem. Slytherin's clearest personal position is also the one that
eventually divided the partnership. Long memory has not treated the four
equally, and a responsible account cannot make equal biographies from unequal
remains.

<!-- evidence: dh-ch16-003, ext-a08-001, ootp-ch11-003, dh-ch29-006, cos-ch09-004 -->

### The Work They Chose Together

What can be said securely begins not with four separate lives but with a joint
decision. The founders sought children who showed signs of magic, brought them
to one place and educated them. Each part mattered. They did not merely offer
instruction to those already within reach; they went looking for pupils. Nor
did they merely gather gifted children; they undertook to teach them.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004 -->

Their collective stature gives this decision weight. Four of the age's most
accomplished magical people committed themselves to the education of the
young. The record does not say who proposed the school, what private motives
were discussed, or in which order agreement was reached. It establishes
something less picturesque and more important: they chose to make education a
shared and continuing work.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Later Hat tradition enlarges this account by remembering a common hope and
calling the founders friends. It further pairs Gryffindor with Slytherin and
Hufflepuff with Ravenclaw. Those pairings may preserve genuine personal
memory, but Binns independently confirms cooperation rather than intimacy.
Friendship should therefore remain tradition, not be made to carry facts about
private conversation or complete agreement.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Whatever the precise warmth of their relations, the partnership endured.
Binns says that the four worked in harmony for several years. Harmony here
need not mean that four powerful minds held identical opinions. It means at
least that their differences did not prevent them from continuing the common
undertaking. A school founded by one person may reflect a single settled will;
Hogwarts began with the harder achievement of sustained cooperation.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004 -->

The limits of the record should not be filled with agreeable scenes. We do not
know where the founders first met, whose invitation brought them together, or
how they divided the work. The site, the raising and concealment of the castle,
the means by which pupils were found, and the arrangements of the first school
belong to the history of the foundation itself. Here the essential fact is the
partnership that made those later questions possible.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Their achievement was not unanimity but common purpose. The founders could
recognise different kinds of promise and still agree that magical children
should be found and taught together. That positive agreement deserves to be
kept in view, for the later dispute has often cast its shadow backwards and
made cooperation look like a brief preface to inevitable failure. Several
years of shared work are evidence against so simple a reading.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

### Four Founders, Four Educational Ideals

The qualities most closely attached to the founders come chiefly through
tradition about the pupils they valued. Gryffindor is associated with courage,
Ravenclaw with exceptional intelligence, Hufflepuff with hard work, and
Slytherin with ambition and cunning. These are remembered preferences, not
complete descriptions of four human beings. A person who values courage may
also think; a person who honours labour need not be without ambition. The
later Houses must not be allowed to swallow their founders whole.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Hufflepuff and Ravenclaw make the first useful comparison. Hat tradition
remembers Hufflepuff as valuing those willing to work and, more strikingly, as
willing to teach the pupils and treat them alike. Ravenclaw is remembered as
preferring the cleverest and the sharpest minds. One emphasis begins with
effort and a broad offer of teaching; the other with intellectual aptitude.
Neither tells us what subject either woman taught or how either conducted a
lesson.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Their traditional friendship does not erase that difference. Nor does
Hufflepuff's remembered willingness establish that every magical child in
Britain was found, admitted or treated equally in practice. It is evidence of
an inclusive founder ideal, and unusually strong evidence by the standards of
her sparse biography, but it is not a complete admissions code for early
Hogwarts. Ravenclaw's friendship with her supplies no missing signature upon
such a code.

<!-- evidence: ootp-ch11-003, cos-ch09-003 -->

The objects attached to them show how selectively a founder can be
remembered. A portrait in Hufflepuff House depicts Helga raising a small,
two-handled golden cup to her students. This establishes a memorial
association within the House, not the cup's physical history or the truth of a
private family's claim to possess it. Ravenclaw's diadem belongs to wider
school lore: it was said to increase wisdom, to have vanished with Ravenclaw
centuries ago, and to have passed beyond living memory. The cup remains an
image of Hufflepuff among pupils; the lost diadem an image of Ravenclaw through
wisdom withheld by loss.

<!-- evidence: ext-a07-005, dh-ch29-006, dh-ch30-003 -->

Gryffindor and Slytherin offer a sharper comparison. Tradition associates
Gryffindor with bravery and Slytherin with shrewdness, ambition and cunning.
None of these qualities is in itself an educational programme, and none need
be treated as a vice. Courage may be reckless, cunning may be prudent, and
ambition may serve a common work. The historical difference between the men
does not lie simply in a contest between a good quality and a bad one.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Gryffindor's sword gives his portrait a little material substance. Later
accounts describe it as an old goblin-made relic bearing his name, and public
historical tradition associates it with worthiness among Gryffindors. The
same body of memory describes Godric as skilled in conventional swordcraft, a
plausible accomplishment in the less separated world before the Statute of
Secrecy. Yet the sword's ownership is contested between wizard and goblin
accounts, and its later marvels cannot be carried backwards into this history.
It is safest here as evidence of Gryffindor's remembered skill and the enduring
judgement attached to his name.

<!-- evidence: ext-a08-001, ext-a08-002, ext-a08-003, ext-a08-004, dh-ch07-006, dh-ch25-002, dh-ch25-003 -->

Slytherin too accumulated the objects and emblems of memory, but his firmest
historical feature is an idea. Binns records that he distrusted pupils of
Muggle parentage and wished magical education restricted to children from
all-magical families. Tradition adds ambition, cunning and power; history more
securely supplies the proposed ancestry threshold. The distinction matters,
because a severe judgement of ability is not the same thing as refusing a
child's claim to be taught on account of parentage.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

The alleged Chamber of Secrets belongs to the same uneven afterlife. It was
known as a Slytherin legend, but Binns expressly separated it from what reliable
history could establish. The legend reveals how Hogwarts came to remember
Slytherin: secretive, threatening and still capable of intruding upon the
school's present. Whether the legend described a real place was not knowledge
available to this history. Its substance, and the later story attached to it,
must wait.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-005 -->

Objects make the four founders visible, but they do not make them whole. A
sword cannot explain Gryffindor's opposition to an ancestry rule; a cup cannot
recover Hufflepuff's teaching; a diadem cannot tell us what Ravenclaw wrote or
read; a legend cannot supply Slytherin's private motives. The educational
ideals survive most usefully when treated as measures of promise: courage,
intelligence, labour and ambition could each direct attention to a different
strength in a pupil.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003, cos-ch09-004, ext-a07-005, ext-a08-001, dh-ch29-006, cos-ch09-005 -->

### The Limits of Agreement

Differences of emphasis need not prevent education in common. One teacher may
notice boldness, another application, another quick understanding and another
the desire to advance. Later Hat tradition remembers such preferences being
accommodated for a time, and Binns independently records several years of
harmony. The evidence does not settle whether every preference was fully
formed at the beginning, but it shows that difference itself was not fatal to
the partnership.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Slytherin's restriction was different in kind. Courage, intelligence, hard
work, ambition and cunning answer the question, *What promise does this pupil
show?* An ancestry rule answers an earlier question: *May this pupil enter at
all?* A child of Muggle parentage might possess any of the qualities the four
founders admired and still fail Slytherin's test before education began.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

This was Slytherin's position, not an established rule of Hogwarts. Binns says
that Slytherin wished admission to be more selective and that serious
disagreement followed; he does not say that the school adopted the proposed
restriction. Later prejudice associated with Slytherin's name cannot be used
as proof that the founders made his doctrine institutional policy.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004 -->

Gryffindor's opposition is clearer than the views of the other two founders.
The serious argument was between Gryffindor and Slytherin. We may therefore say
that Gryffindor rejected Slytherin's ancestry restriction. We may not supply
from that rejection a complete Gryffindor admissions policy, still less assign
the same policy to Ravenclaw. Hufflepuff's broad remembered offer stands on its
own traditional evidence rather than on a reconstructed coalition of three.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Nor can the argument itself be recovered as a scene. No words survive, no date
is secure, and the sources do not tell us whether Slytherin held the position
from the first or whether it hardened during the school's early years. Binns
records disagreements and a serious argument; later Hat tradition remembers a
wider and more violent rupture. The stronger claim is the narrower one. At
this point the history can establish opposition without pretending to have
overheard it.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->

The eventual consequences belong to a later chapter. To end here with the
dispute is not to make it the sum of the founders' work. Before one difference
tested the limits of their agreement, the four had already done what no
surviving account attributes to any one of them alone: they had joined their
abilities to the deliberate education of magical children and sustained that
work together for years.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004 -->

Their partnership could contain distinct judgements of promise; it could not
indefinitely evade the question of who was entitled to learn. Both facts shaped
Hogwarts. Yet the school was not created by the quarrel. It was created by the
common purpose that preceded it. The next task is therefore to turn from the
founders' remembered characters to their practical achievement: how did four
exceptional people make a shared educational purpose into a functioning
school?
