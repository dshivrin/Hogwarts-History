# Chapter Two

## The Four Founders

### Four Names in a Long Memory

From the scattered magical world of the preceding chapter, four names emerge:
Godric Gryffindor, Helga Hufflepuff, Rowena Ravenclaw and Salazar Slytherin.
They were counted among the greatest witches and wizards of their age. Their
distinction matters, but so does what they chose to do with it: they joined
their powers in the education of magical children.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Two kinds of evidence illuminate the founders' early history. Professor Binns
recounts the historical framework of their common undertaking, their years of
harmony and the disagreement that emerged among them; the Sorting Hat preserves
a longstanding school tradition concerning their ideals and friendships.

The four names are much clearer than the four early lives. Godric's Hollow is
recorded as Gryffindor's birthplace. For the others, tradition supplies only
the broad landscapes of valley, glen and fen; it also associates Gryffindor
with a wild moor. These may be old traces of origin, but only Godric's Hollow
can be placed with confidence.

<!-- evidence: dh-ch16-003, gof-ch12-002 -->

### The Work They Chose Together

The founders did more than receive pupils who happened to find them. They
sought out children who showed signs of magic, brought them to one place and
educated them. Four exceptional magical people thus made teaching a deliberate
and continuing enterprise.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004 -->

Binns records that they worked in harmony for several years. The Hat's later
tradition remembers more: a common hope, friendship among all four, and
particular bonds between Gryffindor and Slytherin and between Hufflepuff and
Ravenclaw. Friendship is a more intimate claim than cooperation, and the
pairings give shape to the tradition's memory of their partnership. The songs
do not establish when each founder's preferred standard for pupils first took
form.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

No account of the founders' first meeting survives, nor do we know how they
divided their responsibilities beyond the teaching associated with their
respective Houses.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

### Four Founders, Four Educational Ideals

The founders' remembered preferences concern qualities in pupils rather than
complete theories of education. Gryffindor valued courage; Hufflepuff, hard
work; Ravenclaw, intelligence; Slytherin, ambition and cunning. These ideals
could overlap in any child. They reveal where each founder looked for promise,
not four mutually exclusive kinds of human nature.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Helga Hufflepuff is remembered as willing to teach the pupils whom other
founders might not select and to treat them alike. She honoured hard work, but
did not make it a gate through which only the already industrious might pass.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Later authorial commentary makes a narrower claim about Hufflepuff's treatment
of house-elves. In a 2007 interview preserved in an external transcript,
J. K. Rowling said that Hufflepuff offered them refuge and better working
conditions rather than abolition. The transcript establishes the attribution,
not an independently documented founder-era policy. It neither turns
Hufflepuff into a faultless moral figure nor contradicts the separate tradition
of her pupil-facing ideal, and it does not settle the wider history of
house-elf labour.

Rowena Ravenclaw's ideal was more selective, but no less educational. She is
remembered as seeking the cleverest pupils and the sharpest minds, a standard
that placed intellectual excellence at the centre of a pupil's promise. The
contrast with Hufflepuff is illuminating precisely because tradition remembers
the two women as friends. A separate Hogwarts tradition tells of Ravenclaw's
lost diadem, reputed to enhance the wisdom of its wearer.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003, dh-ch29-006 -->

<!-- source-access: dh-ch29-006 reports longstanding school lore in 1998. Revision 05 uses it as evidence for the existence and limits of that tradition, not as a primary founder-era record. -->

In 1998 Helena Ravenclaw, the Grey Lady, identified herself as Rowena's daughter
and gave a first-person account of the loss. Helena said that she had stolen
her mother's diadem, that Rowena had concealed the loss even from the other
founders, and that the fatally ill Rowena later sought her daughter. Her
testimony supplies evidence about Rowena from within the family, but it remains
one witness's memory, without documentary corroboration from Rowena or another
founder, and it does not disclose Rowena's motives.

Godric Gryffindor is remembered not only for courage but also for martial skill.
A later official account describes him as an accomplished conventional
swordsman in an age when a wizard might defend himself with steel as well as a
wand. The old goblin-made sword bearing his name provides a material counterpart
to that account.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003, ext-a08-001, ext-a08-003 -->

<!-- source-access: ext-a08-001 and ext-a08-003 are later official retrospective accounts of founder-era sword manufacture and swordsmanship. Revision 05 integrates only those limited historical claims; later appearances, Hat mechanics, worthiness rules and the ownership dispute remain reserved. -->

His courage acquires its greatest historical importance in his opposition to
Slytherin. Tradition remembers the two men first as friends. Binns records
Gryffindor as Slytherin's principal opponent when ancestry became a condition
of magical education. The movement from friendship towards disagreement makes
Godric more than the emblem of a preferred virtue. It shows him applying
judgement to the purpose of the institution he had helped create.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Salazar Slytherin shared the work of seeking and educating children and
remained in harmony with his colleagues for years. He was one of the four great
magical people of the age. Tradition associates him with ambition and cunning,
but neither quality explains his motives or supplies achievements the record
does not name.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

Binns reports that Slytherin disliked taking pupils of Muggle parentage, whom
Slytherin regarded as untrustworthy. This was Slytherin's reported belief, not
an established judgement about those children. His preference for magical
ancestry was to become the source of a disagreement that reached beyond the
selection of pupils for his own House.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

### The Limits of Agreement

The Sorting Hat's tradition places ancestry among Slytherin's conditions for
entry into his own House. Binns records a wider demand: that magical education
at Hogwarts should be restricted to children from all-magical families.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, gof-ch12-002, ootp-ch11-003 -->

The restriction remained Slytherin's position; the account does not establish
that Hogwarts adopted it as policy. Gryffindor's opposition is clear, while
Hufflepuff's remembered willingness to teach widely offers a further contrast.
Ravenclaw's complete view of admission does not survive.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->

For several years the founders' differing ideals coexisted within one
undertaking, although the evidence does not establish when those preferences
first arose or became associated with the Houses. The ancestry question was
different in kind: it asked whether some magical children should be excluded
from Hogwarts altogether. That question did not erase the founders' earlier
cooperation, but it exposed the limit of their agreement and made the purpose
of their common project a matter of argument.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, cos-ch09-004, ootp-ch11-003 -->

The founders therefore emerge less as four emblems than as four educators
joined by purpose and divided over its limits. Their shared achievement began
with the decision that young magical people should be taught together; their
disagreement revealed that even a common school could rest on contested ideas
of whom it was for. The next stage of the history is institutional: how that
purpose was made durable in a functioning Hogwarts.
