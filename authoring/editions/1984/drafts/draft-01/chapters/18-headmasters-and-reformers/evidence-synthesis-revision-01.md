## Chapter purpose

Chapter 18 answers the question posed at the end of Chapter 17. Hogwarts's
protections, rules and practices did not recognise their own failure or decide
how to change. The office of Headmaster or Headmistress supplied a centre of
judgement and direction, but the office-holder neither owned the institution
nor acted without limits. The chapter therefore distinguishes the continuing
office, the individual person, delegated staff government, external authority,
and exceptional conditions in which those categories ceased to align neatly.

## Source-access decision

The selected record contains no direct surviving constitutional statement
from a reconstructed 1984 edition. The deepest history of the office is later
carried: repeated searches are attributed to successive heads; Dilys Derwent's
portrait supplies eighteenth-century dates; official retrospective accounts
describe the school-owned Pensieve and the traditional preparation of
headmasterial portraits. These establish an old and continuing office, but not
its founding date, original form or normal appointment process.

The best pre-1984 case studies are also later carried. Dippet's authority is
seen through Riddle's diary memory, Riddle's confession and Dumbledore's later
commentary. Dumbledore's admission of Lupin, role in Hagrid's retention and
refusal of Voldemort's teaching request concern pre-1984 events revealed only
later. They may enter a unified history with explicit source status; they may
not be silently converted into Bagshot's demonstrated knowledge.

Post-1984 evidence serves two functions. Some scenes reveal practices that may
be older—deputy-headmistress correspondence, portrait service, office
succession—but do not date their origin. Other scenes are later history in
their own right: staff appointments, Dementor security, governor suspension,
the Umbridge decrees, wartime appointment of Snape, and McGonagall's emergency
leadership. These later conflicts provide the clearest evidence of boundaries,
precisely because the boundaries were being contested. Their exceptional
visibility is not evidence of ordinary earlier practice.

## Executive finding

The evidence supports a continuing office with substantial practical command
over Hogwarts's internal life. Named heads admitted or retained pupils,
decided exceptional residence, appointed or refused staff, accepted or refused
resignations, imposed probation and school-wide prohibitions, controlled
access, altered the operation of protective enchantments, directed emergency
responses and represented the school to outsiders. No selected source gathers
these acts into a comprehensive legal grant, and not every act is established
for every head.

The office was exercised through other staff. Heads of House made disciplinary
decisions, a Deputy Headmistress handled correspondence and crises, and senior
staff deliberated upon closure and evacuation. A head could direct the school
without personally administering every part of it.

The office also had superiors and rivals. Governors invoked appointment and
suspension authority, recalled a suspended head and were later treated as the
body making the final reopening decision. The Ministry could remove a person,
alter a pupil's status, legislate new internal offices and eventually appoint
or confirm heads under exceptional regimes. Yet the limits were reciprocal:
Dumbledore could negotiate Ministry discipline, exclude Dementors from the
castle, retain residential authority after a Ministry-backed dismissal and
make an appointment before the Ministry's conditional power arose.

Individual judgement mattered. Dippet and Dumbledore inherited recognisably
the same kind of office but used it differently. Dumbledore's unusual
accommodations, staffing choices and political independence reveal what a
determined head could do, while his reputation and outside offices prevent all
deference to him from being attributed to the headship. Umbridge and Snape
show the converse: holding the title did not guarantee the same independence,
legitimacy or practical command.

The strongest supported conclusion is therefore that Hogwarts survived its
heads without remaining unaffected by them. The institution was older than
each custodian, but its rules, staff, admissions, tolerances and relationship
with the state could change through the judgement of the person temporarily
entrusted with it.
