# Chapter 18 — Headmasters and Headmistresses

A wall may bar a passage and an enchantment may conceal a castle, but neither
can decide that a danger has become intolerable. Such judgement belongs to
people. At Hogwarts it has fallen most visibly to the Headmaster or
Headmistress: the person expected to admit or exclude, appoint or refuse,
tolerate a risk or alter the school in answer to it. The history of Hogwarts's
defences therefore leads naturally to the history of an office whose principal
instrument was decision.

That office was powerful without being absolute. Its holder directed the
school but did not perform all its government, could alter practice without
ensuring permanence, and might command great loyalty without becoming
Hogwarts itself. The distinction is easiest to lose in the case of a celebrated
head and easiest to see when authority was disputed. What belonged to the
office, what belonged to the person, and what remained in other hands are
different questions.

The headship was already an old office by the time its history becomes clear
enough to name particular holders. Professor Binns later spoke of successive
heads searching in vain for the Chamber of Secrets, while later records
preserve former heads as a recognised group. This establishes succession and
institutional memory, not an unbroken roll. The first holder, the first method
of appointment and the point at which the office acquired its later shape all
remain beyond the surviving record.

The first Headmistress whom the surviving record securely dates is Dilys
Derwent, who held the post from 1741 until 1768. The qualification matters.
She is the earliest whom that record allows us to place securely, not the first
woman to govern Hogwarts. Everard and Phineas Nigellus Black can also be
identified as former heads, but isolated names do not close the intervals
between them or recover the order of every succession.

The office nevertheless accumulated ways of addressing its own past. A school
Pensieve was later said to contain memories left by a long succession of heads,
and portraits of former heads could be prepared in life with knowledge useful
to those who followed. In later crises Dumbledore sent the portraits of
Everard and Dilys to their other frames to observe and report. Memory,
representation and advice thus passed across headships, but the objects and
portraits belonged to the institution which successive heads served. The
incumbent inherited them; he did not own the past they preserved.

Continuity of this sort says little about constitutional uniformity. A title
may endure while the means of acquiring it, the powers attached to it and the
authorities surrounding it change. The safer measure is therefore practical:
what decisions could a known head make, whom did those decisions bind, and what
happened when somebody resisted?

One unusually durable decision arose from an altercation with Peeves in 1876.
Headmistress Eupraxia Mole negotiated his surrender and accepted terms which
included privileges and material concessions for the poltergeist. The details
belong to the strange history of Peeves; the point here is the settlement's
institutional force. A Headmistress could conclude an agreement which governed
how Hogwarts dealt with a persistent inhabitant after the immediate disorder
had ended.

Armando Dippet used other instruments. After a Christmas pantomime ended in
fire, injury and evacuation, he placed Professor Kettleburn on probation and
prohibited future pantomimes. The first decision attached continuing judgement
to an individual; the second converted a particular failure into a rule for
the school. Neither proves an unlimited power to dismiss staff or legislate on
every matter. Together they show authority reaching beyond the moment which
provoked it.

Kettleburn is said to have accumulated no fewer than sixty-two periods of
probation, most under Dippet, without losing his post. Dumbledore later treated
him more indulgently. The contrast is useful precisely because the office is
the same while its exercise differs. Probation supplied both heads with a means
of supervision short of removal; temperament and judgement affected how that
means was used.

Dippet's headship is most revealing under the pressure of 1943. During the
attacks at Hogwarts, Tom Riddle asked to remain at school for the summer rather
than return to his orphanage. Dippet did not treat residence as an automatic
entitlement. He considered the request, recognised that special arrangements
might otherwise have been possible, and refused because a pupil had died and
further attacks were feared.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch13-005, cos-ch13-007 -->
<!-- source-access: The exchange is preserved in Riddle's diary memory, which is an interested later-accessed source. It establishes Dippet's consideration and refusal, not an ordinary summer-residence policy. -->

He also told Riddle that the Ministry of Magic was considering whether
Hogwarts should close. This placed his decision within a pressure larger than
one pupil's accommodation. Dippet could refuse residence; the account does not
say that he alone could close the school, nor that the Ministry had already
issued an order. The danger had made the continued operation of Hogwarts a
question shared, or at least contested, beyond the headmaster's study.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch13-007 -->
<!-- source-access: The diary memory establishes Ministry discussion and Dippet's residence decision. It does not preserve the legal allocation of closure authority or a completed closure. -->

Riddle then presented Hagrid as the cause of the attacks. The accusation was
accepted sufficiently for Hagrid to be expelled and his wand broken, but
Riddle's later confession reveals that he had framed him. The surviving
accounts do not identify who formally ordered either sanction, what inquiry
was held, or what evidence was heard. Dippet governed amid deception; that
fact does not entitle a later history to supply a procedure which the record
withholds.

What followed can be divided more securely. Dumbledore, then the
Transfiguration teacher, suspected Hagrid's innocence and persuaded Headmaster
Dippet to retain and train him as gamekeeper. Hagrid's own compressed account
later credited Dumbledore with allowing him to remain, but the fuller relation
preserves the offices involved: Dumbledore supplied advocacy and Dippet the
headmasterial decision. Influence could alter an outcome without becoming the
formal authority by which it was altered.

<!-- evidence: ps-ch04-007, cos-ch17-003 -->
<!-- source-access: Hagrid's participant summary compresses causation; Riddle's confession states that Dumbledore persuaded Dippet. The prose assigns initiative and formal position accordingly without claiming a reversal of expulsion or wand-breaking. -->

Dippet also refused Riddle's request for a teaching post after he left school,
giving his youth as the reason and allowing that he might apply again. That
decision, the summer refusal and the outcome secured for Hagrid concerned
different kinds of belonging: pupil residence, continued institutional service
after expulsion, and entry to the staff. They show Dippet exercising practical
judgement over access to Hogwarts, not administering a complete employment or
disciplinary code now available to us.

No verdict upon Dippet's character is required to see the institutional
pattern. He made consequential decisions while Ministry pressure, a colleague's
counsel and a pupil's deceit narrowed or distorted what he could know. The
office made his judgement effective; it did not make his information complete.

Dumbledore's later headship shows more vividly how one person's judgement could
alter the institution he inherited. When Remus Lupin was of school age,
Dumbledore admitted him despite the danger attached to his monthly
transformation. Admission required more than an exception written upon a roll.
The Whomping Willow was planted, a concealed route provided and an isolated
building used so that Lupin could attend Hogwarts without exposing the castle
to the full risk of his condition.

<!-- evidence: poa-ch18-003 -->
<!-- source-access: Lupin's pre-1984 admission and accommodation are known through later participant testimony. The evidence supports Dumbledore's decision and the physical arrangement, not its cost, every participant or a universal admissions principle. -->

The arrangement displays headmasterial discretion at its most transformative.
Hogwarts did not merely tolerate a pupil whom ordinary expectation would have
excluded; it changed its physical practice around him. Yet after another pupil
deliberately breached the secret and nearly caused a death, the recorded
response was a demand for silence, not a new design or Lupin's expulsion. The
case establishes a decision and the risks Dumbledore accepted, not a permanent
policy for every comparable pupil.

<!-- evidence: poa-ch18-003, poa-ch18-007 -->
<!-- source-access: The accommodation and later breach are participant accounts disclosed after 1984. Chapter 17 owns the containment analysis; here the incident demonstrates a head's inclusive judgement and its recorded limit. -->

Hagrid reveals the continuity of that judgement across two different positions.
As a teacher Dumbledore persuaded Dippet to preserve Hagrid's place at the
school; as Headmaster he later gave Hagrid teaching duties. The first act
depended upon influence with the holder of the office, the second upon
Dumbledore's own practical appointment authority. The same preference for
institutional inclusion worked through unequal powers.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch17-003, poa-ch05-007, ps-ch04-007 -->

Appointment was not simply a reward for sympathy. Dumbledore knowingly hired
Lupin despite resistance from other staff, but refused the later teaching
application of the man once known as Riddle. In both cases he judged the access
to pupils which a teaching post would confer. The opposite results show the
weight of personal assessment; they do not disclose a written test of
eligibility or prove that no other authority ever participated.

<!-- evidence: poa-ch17-003, hbp-ch20-007 -->

Later announcements make the practice visible across several appointments.
Dumbledore presented Lupin and Hagrid to the school, and in another year
announced Slughorn and Snape in changed subject posts. Hostile publicity could
describe Hagrid as Dumbledore's appointment because the association was
publicly intelligible. These examples establish a broad working authority over
staffing; their variety is more reliable than any claim that the head alone
controlled every contract, consultation or dismissal.

The distinction between appointment and retention appeared when Hagrid tried
to resign amid press attack and parental complaints. Dumbledore set supportive
letters against hostile ones, rejected universal popularity as a sensible
standard and refused the resignation. Public indignation did not automatically
govern the staff. This one refusal does not establish that every resignation
required acceptance, but it shows a head treating continued service as a
matter for judgement rather than counting the loudest owls.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch24-007 -->
<!-- source-access: The 1995 resignation episode is later editorial evidence. It establishes Dumbledore's decision in one case, not a general law of tenure or compulsory service. -->

Dumbledore could therefore make Hogwarts hospitable to people whom another
head might have excluded, and keep them there when opposition arose. It would
be a mistake, however, to rename these decisions as an impersonal programme of
the office. They reveal what this Headmaster chose to do with substantial
discretion. They do not show that his successor was bound to preserve every
appointment or accommodation.

<!-- evidence: poa-ch18-003, cos-ch17-003, poa-ch17-003, gof-ch24-007 -->

The difficulty of separating person from office grows greater in Dumbledore's
case because he brought authority to Hogwarts as well as drawing authority
from it. His public achievements and wider offices preceded or accompanied his
headship, and Hagrid believed that Voldemort's fear of him helped make the
school safe during the first war. Later, the Minister himself complained that
Dumbledore had been allowed wide latitude over staff and teaching. Reputation,
political confidence and formal office reinforced one another without becoming
the same thing.

<!-- evidence: ps-ch06-003, gof-ch36-005 -->
<!-- source-access: Dumbledore's achievements are later-carried biography and Hagrid's statement is participant testimony; Fudge's account is hostile political evidence from 1995. Personal deterrence and tolerated latitude are not formal powers of headship. -->

His control of access and school practice was nevertheless concrete. He
refused Dementors entry to the castle during the search for Sirius Black, and
later suspended the anti-Apparition restriction within the Great Hall for one
hour of supervised teaching. One decision limited an outside force; the other
made a precise exception to an internal protection. Both demonstrate a
powerful incumbent. Neither tells us that every head could command the same
obedience or make the same magic yield.

<!-- evidence: poa-ch09-004, hbp-ch18-003 -->
<!-- source-access: These are post-1984 decisions by Dumbledore. They establish practical control in two circumstances, not the legal basis, ancient continuity or universal extent of the office. -->

Headship of such breadth still depended upon government through others.
McGonagall was named Deputy Headmistress in school correspondence, a title that
plainly marked senior authority without preserving a complete description of
the post. When two pupils arrived by flying car, Dumbledore set the warning
against repetition and undertook to write to their families, while McGonagall,
acting as Head of House, imposed the immediate punishment. The Headmaster
directed the institutional response without personally making every decision.

<!-- evidence: ps-ch04-002, cos-ch05-005 -->
<!-- source-access: McGonagall's title and the 1992 division of responsibility are later observations. One incident does not supply a universal deputy or disciplinary code. -->

The same distribution became clearer in absence. During a later crisis,
McGonagall received urgent school business because Dumbledore was away. After
his death, she occupied the headmaster's office and summoned the Heads of
Houses. A pupil inferred from her deputyship that she was now Headmistress.
The sequence shows practical continuity through a senior colleague, but an
inference made in emergency is not evidence of an automatic law of succession.

<!-- evidence: hbp-ch12-001, hbp-ch29-005 -->

Nor did occupying the office free her from consultation. McGonagall gathered
the Heads of Houses to consider closure and acknowledged that the governors
held the final decision about reopening. In the last battle she again worked
through colleagues, assigning the evacuation of pupils while other staff took
separate responsibilities. Central authority remained real because it could
coordinate distributed action, not because it swallowed every lesser office.

The governors supplied the clearest formal limit upon a head. In 1993 Lucius
Malfoy produced an Order of Suspension signed by all twelve members of the
Board, and the Minister accepted that the appointment or suspension of a
headmaster was the governors' affair. Dumbledore left the school. The incident
demonstrates a mechanism capable of separating an incumbent from the office;
it does not reveal when the Board acquired that power or whether this was its
ordinary procedure.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch14-005, cos-ch14-006 -->
<!-- source-access: The signed order and Fudge's response belong to the 1993 crisis. They establish the invoked governor mechanism, not its origin, stable membership or ordinary use. -->

Its formal appearance must be read beside the pressure behind it. Hagrid said
that Malfoy had threatened governors and their families; Dumbledore later gave
a similar account of the coerced signatures. Suspension was therefore possible,
but the best-documented exercise of it was corrupted. A power may be genuine
even when a particular use of it is neither independent nor wise.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch14-005, cos-ch14-006, beedle-ch02-009 -->
<!-- source-access: Coercion is reported by Hagrid and later by Dumbledore. The prose distinguishes evidence of Board capacity from the legitimacy of this suspension. -->

Dumbledore's response exposes the opposite distinction. Although suspended,
he predicted that those loyal to him would still seek his help. That was not a
constitutional privilege of the displaced Headmaster; it was personal
authority surviving the loss of formal position. The school could remove him
from office more readily than it could remove his relationships from the
school.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch14-007 -->
<!-- source-access: Dumbledore's statement is made during the 1993 suspension. It supports continuing personal influence and loyalty, not a formal power retained by every suspended head. -->

The other governors eventually recalled him, and Malfoy was removed from the
Board. These reversals establish practical powers of recall and of ending a
governor's service without disclosing their procedures. Dumbledore later placed
the episode within a longer political struggle, but his interpretation does
not make the governors a permanent enemy of Hogwarts. Their office constrained
the Headmaster even when one governor had abused it.

Ministry authority reached the headship by a different route. Dippet's warning
of possible closure in 1943 shows outside pressure entering a school crisis,
but not its legal conclusion. In 1995 a Ministry notice expelling Harry Potter
and ordering the destruction of his wand was revised after Dumbledore's
intervention to suspension pending a hearing. The Headmaster did not nullify
the state by speaking; neither did the first state decision remain beyond
challenge.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch13-007, ootp-ch02-003 -->

Fudge's later complaint that Dumbledore had enjoyed free rein over staffing
and teaching is best understood as a hostile admission of practical autonomy.
It was made when political confidence had broken down and closer control was
being threatened. The grievance shows that latitude could be broad while the
Ministry tolerated it, and vulnerable when it did not. It is evidence of a
working relationship, not a charter of permanent independence.

Dolores Umbridge's rise makes the subsequent change unusually visible because
her powers arrived in stages. She first entered Hogwarts as a teacher through
Ministry intervention. Educational Decrees then created the unprecedented
office of High Inquisitor, with powers to inspect the staff, before a still
later decree named her Head of Hogwarts. Teacher, inquisitor and head were not
three names for one authority; they were successive offices with distinct
sources of power.

<!-- evidence: ootp-ch15-001, ootp-ch28-001 -->
<!-- source-access: The Ministry appointment mechanism, first High Inquisitor and later designation as Head of Hogwarts are documented post-1984 changes. They are not projected backwards into ordinary headship. -->

As High Inquisitor, Umbridge acquired power to alter punishments imposed by
other members of staff. This did more than place an inspector inside the
castle. It rewrote the relation between an established school office and a new
Ministry one, allowing the latter to override discipline which had formerly
been exercised within the ordinary staff hierarchy. The power belonged to the
decree-created inquisitorship, not to teaching in general and not yet to
headship.

The dismissal of Professor Trelawney divided authority even more precisely.
Umbridge could remove her from the teaching post, but Dumbledore denied that
dismissal carried the power to expel her from the castle. He then appointed
Firenze before the Ministry's conditional appointment power could operate,
because that power arose only if the Headmaster failed to find a candidate.
In one encounter, dismissal, residence and replacement belonged to different
and overlapping claims.

Only after Dumbledore's departure was Umbridge appointed Head of Hogwarts.
Even then, the Headmaster's office reportedly sealed itself against her. A
room's refusal cannot settle the legality of an appointment, but it gives the
institutional difficulty a wonderfully Hogwarts character. Formal designation
had not instantly conferred the access, acceptance or practical command which
Dumbledore had possessed.

<!-- evidence: ootp-ch28-001 -->

The Umbridge year is consequently revealing because it was abnormal. Ministry
intervention separated powers which ordinary practice had allowed a head to
exercise together, then assembled others in newly created offices. The result
does not disclose how Hogwarts had always been governed. It shows how quickly
headship could be fragmented when an outside authority began assigning its
functions one decree at a time.

Wartime headship placed the distinction between title and command under still
greater strain. In 1997 the public announcement of Severus Snape's appointment
as Headmaster appeared beside the installation of the Carrows at Hogwarts.
The title is certain; the appointing process and the independent extent of his
authority are not. He governed within a school already subjected to a regime
whose power did not originate in its staff.

<!-- evidence: dh-ch12-001 -->

A private obligation pulled in another direction. Dumbledore had made Snape
promise to protect the pupils if Voldemort gained control of Hogwarts. The
promise explains why the public holder of an occupied office might not be
wholly identified with the regime around him. It does not establish that every
later act was protective, that Snape was free to act as he wished, or that a
private promise supplied lawful authority.

<!-- evidence: dh-ch33-006 -->
<!-- source-access: The promise is disclosed through later private memory. It establishes a concealed obligation and divided allegiance, not the motive or effect of every act during Snape's headship. -->

His tenure ended in practice before the record supplies any formal removal.
McGonagall, Flitwick and Sprout resisted him inside the school, and Snape fled.
No order of dismissal, governor vote or Ministry instrument appears in the
account. A headship could therefore cease as an effective command when the
staff and circumstances which made command possible turned against its holder.

Snape and Umbridge reveal fragmentation by external power; Dumbledore presents
the reverse difficulty, for his personal standing enlarged the apparent reach
of the office. Dippet occupied the same recognisable headship while exercising
it under different pressures and with different information. Equal titles did
not produce equal independence, judgement, legitimacy or effect. The office
made each holder consequential, but it did not make them interchangeable.

The head directed Hogwarts but did not own it. A head could settle a dispute,
prohibit an activity, admit an exceptional pupil, choose among teachers and
limit access to the castle. Yet staff carried delegated authority, governors
could remove an incumbent, the Ministry could negotiate or intervene, and war
could leave the title detached from secure command. Power at Hogwarts was
substantial precisely because these acts mattered; it was limited because
other people and offices could determine whether they endured.

<!-- evidence: ext-a16-005, beedle-ch02-005, poa-ch18-003, cos-ch05-005, cos-ch14-006, ootp-ch26-006, dh-ch30-005 -->

The consequences were concrete. Successive heads changed who could remain at
Hogwarts, whom it employed, what risks it accepted, which practices it
prohibited and how far external authority entered the castle. Not every
decision became permanent and not every difference amounted to improvement.
The institution was nevertheless altered whenever a judgement opened a place,
closed a possibility or shifted the boundary at which another power could act.

<!-- evidence: beedle-ch02-005, cos-ch17-003, poa-ch18-003, hbp-ch20-007, ootp-ch15-001 -->

Hogwarts survived each Headmaster and Headmistress because the school, its
staff and the office itself continued beyond one tenure. Survival did not mean
remaining untouched. Each custodian inherited earlier judgements, exercised
authority within the pressures of a particular time and left consequences for
someone else to govern. The person was temporary; the effects need not be.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-001, ext-a05-003, beedle-ch02-005, poa-ch18-003, cos-ch14-006 -->

By the modern age, those accumulated choices had become part of the school
which later heads received. So had the contests around them: between judgement
and rule, personal influence and formal office, school government and public
authority. An ancient institution did not enter modernity merely by lasting.
It entered carrying the decisions of its temporary custodians and facing new
pressures upon the arrangements they had made.
