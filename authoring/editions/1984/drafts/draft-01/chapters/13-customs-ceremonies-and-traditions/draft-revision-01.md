# Chapter 13 — Customs, Ceremonies and Traditions

The Sorting Hat carried an old judgement into each new school year, but the Hat
did not perform alone. Children waited, entered, listened, were named and moved
to tables at which older pupils were ready to receive them. The institution was
renewed not simply because something ancient had survived, but because a new
company submitted itself to a recognisable sequence. Memory had supplied the
voice. Ceremony supplied the listeners, the witnesses and the next generation
upon whom that memory would act.

This distinction matters because Hogwarts held both customs and ceremonies,
and the two words are not interchangeable. Repeated practice is evidence of
custom. A single formal occasion may instead show the school's capacity to
construct ceremony when circumstance requires it. The arrival of first-years,
the opening feast and the House Cup belonged to a recurring rhythm in the later
school. A tournament welcome, a funeral or a hall dressed for mourning could
be no less solemn, yet repetition is not proved merely by formality. Hogwarts
could inherit, repeat, alter and invent; its history lies partly in knowing
which of these it was doing.

The school year began before the castle appeared. A later history of the
Hogwarts Express connected collective transport with the demands of secrecy
after the Statute of 1692. Earlier families had brought children by various
means; eventually a Ministry-approved train became compulsory. The account
attributes the railway solution to Minister Ottaline Gambol, though it also
preserves uncertainty about exactly how the locomotive was acquired. The point
for Hogwarts custom is narrower than the history of the train. By the later
school, pupils began and ended the year through a common route, at a common
hour, rather than arriving as separate families at the castle door.

At Hogsmeade the common journey divided. In 1991 Hagrid called the first-years
together while luggage was taken separately, and led them down to small boats.
Their approach carried them across the dark lake, through a curtain of ivy and
into a harbour beneath the castle. Most importantly, the water gave the new
pupils their first full sight of Hogwarts: towers and windows raised above the
cliff before any corridor or classroom had reduced the castle to daily use.
Later accounts explicitly called this the traditional first-year route.

Returning pupils did not repeat that approach. They went by stagecoach, while
the newcomers crossed together and apart from the established school. No
source says that the founders designed the journey as an initiation, or even
that its visual effect was its intended purpose. Yet it functioned as one. The
first-years were separated by status, shown the castle as a whole, and admitted
by a route the older pupils no longer used. Hogwarts made a distinction between
those who already belonged and those who were about to be received.

The separation continued inside. Before the 1991 Sorting, Professor McGonagall
kept the first-years apart from the Hall and explained that the ceremony would
place them in Houses whose members would become their school community. They
then entered before pupils and staff already seated. The arrangement made
admission public. A new pupil did not learn a private result and report to a
dormitory afterwards; the school watched the membership of its four permanent
communities being renewed.

By 1994 the mature procedure can be described in greater detail. A stool and
the Hat stood before the school; a scroll supplied the names; each child was
called forward, seated and publicly assigned; the receiving table answered
with applause. The surviving evidence supports names read from a roll but does
not establish alphabetical calling as an ancient rule, still less the age of
the stool, scroll or exact procession. What the ceremony securely accomplished
was social. It turned a group of arrivals into named members, and followed the
verdict with a place among those to whom each now belonged.

The Hat's song preceded those decisions. Under the settled school tradition it
was an annual performance, newly composed rather than mechanically repeated.
Later testimony held that in dangerous periods the Hat had used its song to
warn the school, and a wartime performance again pressed the need for unity.
The custom therefore joined continuity to variation: the same inherited voice
could address a different company and a different danger. Its institutional
authority came partly from recurrence, but recurrence did not require it to say
the same thing.

Sorting passed directly into the opening feast. House tables converted the
announcement into company: the child just assigned was fed beside the pupils
who had applauded. The whole school was present, yet it was present in four
communities, with the staff facing them and ghosts moving among them in the
later Hall. Food mattered less here as abundance than as sequence. Judgement
ended, places were taken, and the school ate together before its officers
declared the conditions of the year.

Those declarations formed a custom of their own. Later pupils recognised the
start-of-term notices as usual: warnings about forbidden places and objects,
introductions of staff, and announcements of activities. The form remained
recognisable precisely because the content could change. In 1994 the familiar
rules were followed by the exceptional cancellation of the Quidditch Cup; in
wartime the address answered different dangers. Repetition supplied a place in
which novelty could be made official. Hogwarts did not need a new ceremony for
every new condition. It altered what was said within an existing one.

At the 1991 feast, institutional speech gave way to institutional song.
Dumbledore summoned the words of the school song above the tables in a golden
ribbon and invited everyone to choose a favourite tune. The result was neither
musical uniformity nor private performance: the school sang the same words,
finished at different times and waited while the last two voices completed a
funeral march under Dumbledore's conducting wand. This single scene does not
prove that the song was sung every September, or that every performance allowed
the same liberty. It nevertheless reveals a fitting contrast. The Hat sang as
an inherited voice to the school; the school song existed only when the present
company joined in, together and distinctly themselves.

The Great Hall repeatedly made such acts visible to the whole institution. It
received the newly Sorted, carried announcements, held seasonal feasts and
displayed the outcome of House competition. This was not merely a consequence
of size, nor does it require another account of the enchanted ceiling or the
room's construction. Gathering converted private experiences into common
knowledge. A pupil might receive points in one corridor or spend Christmas
with a small circle of friends; when the Hall changed its colours, its seating
or its decorations, Hogwarts presented an interpretation of the occasion to
everyone present.

Halloween supplies the clearest repeated seasonal example. In three successive
years the Great Hall held a decorated feast. The second of those accounts calls
the live bats usual and again records pumpkins; the third includes ghostly
entertainment in which spectral horsemen and a beheaded performer formed part
of the evening. The recurrence of the feast and decoration is secure for those
years. The programme is less so. One ghost performance does not create an
annual entertainment rule, and the emergencies that happened to interrupt
Halloween gatherings belong to the history of those emergencies, not to the
festival.

Christmas left a broader trail of repeated preparation. Lists were circulated
for pupils who would remain at school. Twelve trees stood in the Hall in 1991,
and later accounts called both their number and the wider decoration usual.
Staff took part in the work; enchanted snow and carols appear in one year, and
Hagrid is later seen delivering the trees. These particulars should
not be hardened into an unchanging programme. Together they show a recognised
school observance assembled by practical labour as well as magic.

The most revealing Christmas arrangement occurred when few pupils remained.
Instead of preserving the four House tables for a diminished company, staff
and pupils sat at one shared table. The feast continued while its ordinary
social geometry was set aside. This was not a failure of tradition but an
example of how tradition survived: the school kept the communal occasion by
altering the arrangement through which it was expressed. The form that mattered
was gathering, not the compulsory preservation of every piece of furniture in
its usual relation.

Easter is more modest in the evidence. Pupils could remain at Hogwarts during
the holiday, and by a later examination year the break had become a period of
revision. The surviving accounts establish no distinct Hogwarts Easter feast,
magical observance or ceremonial assembly. The silence should
not be filled from the ordinary existence of a British holiday. In the known
school calendar, Easter is securely a break and a hinge towards examinations;
anything more remains unproved.

House competition gave that calendar a running total. In the later school,
giant hourglasses made points visible, so that rewards and penalties accumulated
before the eyes of the community they affected. The system translated acts by
individual pupils into the standing of a larger group. Its full administration
belongs to the history of school authority, and the evidence does not justify
a universal list of who might change the totals or by how much. Its ceremonial
importance lies in the public passage from individual act to collective score.

At the end-of-year feast those dispersed additions and deductions received a
public resolution. Final standings were announced, the House Cup awarded and
the Hall dressed in the colours of the winner. A later feast repeated the
winning-House display after examination results. The conclusion could still be
surprising: exceptional awards might alter the result, and service during a
crisis could bring honours and unusually large totals. Ceremony did not make
the process impersonal. It made the judgement visible, attached it to a trophy
and colours, and allowed one House's success to become the school's closing
spectacle.

Departure then reversed arrival. In 1992 pupils received holiday warnings,
crossed the lake, boarded the Express, changed out of robes and passed through
the barrier in controlled groups. The following year ended differently after
a crisis—examinations were cancelled and celebration expanded—but the train
still carried pupils away. The evidence does not show that every part of the
1992 sequence was universal. It does show the later school year closing through
another managed movement from castle to home. The cohort admitted together in
September was returned at summer's beginning, no longer new to the institution
whose cycle it had completed.

Most of these customs enter the record already in operation. Their origins are
unknown, and recurrence must be recovered from several years of observation.
A Christmas pantomime mounted during Armando Dippet's headship is different.
Albus Dumbledore's later recollection describes it expressly as Professor
Herbert Beery's attempt to introduce such performances into Hogwarts
festivities. It was the school's sole attempt. Here, unusually, the evidence
does not merely show a tradition encountered after its beginnings had been
forgotten. It shows a proposed tradition at the moment of trial.

Beery chose *The Fountain of Fair Fortune*. A young Dumbledore, then teaching
Transfiguration, supplied magical effects. Silvanus Kettleburn supplied an
enlarged Ashwinder for the stage. During the performance the creature laid eggs
among the scenery; fire followed, while two performers duelled over the same
part and the Great Hall had to be evacuated through smoke. The hospital wing
was packed, and Beery later left Hogwarts to teach at W.A.D.A. Another official
account corroborated Kettleburn's creature and the resulting fire, though not
every detail of Dumbledore's narrative.

<!-- evidence: beedle-ch02-002, beedle-ch02-003, beedle-ch02-004, beedle-ch02-005, ext-a13-003 -->

Dippet's response was to prohibit future pantomimes. According to the same
recollection, the prohibition endured. Beery's entertainment therefore entered
Hogwarts history by failing to enter its calendar. The performance was not a
custom; the refusal to repeat it became one. This episode exposes a process
hidden behind older practices. Traditions are selected as well as inherited.
Some survive because a community repeats an occasion; this one survived in the
negative, because authority remembered an occasion too vividly to permit its
return.

Exceptional occasions reveal the opposite capacity. In 1994 Hogwarts received
the delegations of Beauxbatons and Durmstrang by shortening lessons, assembling
outside and conducting the visitors into a welcoming feast. Champion selection
then became a public Halloween ceremony before the gathered schools. These
events belonged to a revived inter-school tournament, not to the ordinary
Hogwarts year. Yet the school accommodated them by extending familiar habits:
formal arrival, collective witness, the Hall and the headmaster's public voice.

The Yule Ball made the distinction still clearer. It was described as a
traditional part of the Triwizard Tournament, not as an annual Hogwarts dance.
Champions opened it as representatives; a procession and judges' table replaced
the ordinary relation of House tables in the transformed Hall. Hogwarts was
therefore participating in a tradition larger than itself and temporarily
rearranging its own forms to do so. One revived ball cannot establish the
layout or practice of earlier tournaments, but it shows the school borrowing a
ceremonial obligation and making room for it within its existing centre of
assembly.

Ceremony could also answer a circumstance for which celebration was no longer
adequate. After Cedric Diggory's death, the Leaving Feast did not display the
colours of the House Cup winner. Black draperies covered the Hall instead, and
the gathering became an act of mourning. The alteration gained force from the
ordinary custom it displaced. Hogwarts did not abandon the final assembly; it
changed what the room said. The same population, place and position in the
calendar were used to acknowledge that the year could not close as usual.

Dumbledore's funeral two years later was more singular still. Pupils moved by
House to seats beside the lake, visitors joined them, and the departure of the
Hogwarts Express was delayed around the observance. Nothing in that event
establishes a standing funeral rite for headmasters. Its importance here is
precisely the absence of such proof. When ordinary rhythm was insufficient,
Hogwarts could organise a new schoolwide ceremony from familiar elements:
ordered movement, collective attendance, a significant place and an altered
timetable.

The history of Hogwarts custom is consequently not a history of forms kept
untouched. The Hat changed its song. Opening notices changed their warning.
Christmas contracted four tables into one. A House-coloured feast yielded to
black drapery. A tournament inserted foreign processions and a ball into the
school's calendar, while a disastrous pantomime left behind a prohibition
rather than an annual performance. Continuity lay in the school's ability to
make altered circumstances legible through acts its members performed
together.

Every September another group approached the castle, waited outside the Hall,
heard the inherited song and took its places among communities older than
itself. Through the year those pupils joined feasts, watched points rise and
fall, saw the Hall answer season and circumstance, and finally departed after
the school's competition had been given its public ending. Tradition made
institutional history participatory: not merely something pupils were told had
happened, but something they were required, invited or permitted to do again.
Among those repetitions, none gathered House identity into a more visible
season of its own than Quidditch.
