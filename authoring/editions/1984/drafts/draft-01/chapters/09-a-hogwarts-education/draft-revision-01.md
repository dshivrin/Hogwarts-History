# Chapter 9 — A Hogwarts Education

Authority becomes education at the moment a school tells a child not merely
that a place is offered, but what must be brought to fill it. A later Hogwarts
acceptance letter did both. It named the school, gave the opening of term and a
date by which an answer was expected, then directed the new pupil to an
enclosed list of books and equipment. Admission was therefore not left as a
bare discovery of magical ability. It was converted into preparation for
study.

The magical instruments that selected prospective pupils did not themselves
perform that work. The Book of Admittance and Quill of Acceptance could decide
whose name belonged in the school record; an invitation still had to be sent.
One preserved account says that the register was checked each year and owls
were dispatched to children approaching eleven. That account says the Quill
detected magical birth, whereas a later official account makes a child's first
display of magic the trigger. The disagreement need not be tidied away. Both
describe an enchanted register followed by a human act of notification; they
do not agree on the precise beginning of the record.

Eleven was thus the usual threshold at which private, uncontrolled magic gave
way to schooling. The surviving letter expected a reply and announced a
September beginning; an earlier childhood memory adds that owl post was normal
but not always sufficient. A Muggle-born child's parents, it was said, would
receive an explanation from someone at the school. This is not evidence for an
elaborate admissions office. It is evidence for a practical distinction:
families already acquainted with magic could read an owl-delivered invitation,
while others might first need Hogwarts itself explained to them.

Later testimony describes the education that followed as seven years long.
The first of those years began with unusually material demands: work robes and
protective gloves; a wand, cauldron, phials, telescope and scales; and eight
named books. The same list reminded parents that first-years might learn to fly
but might not own broomsticks at school. Before a pupil had entered a classroom,
Hogwarts had therefore marked out both the instruments of study and one limit
upon their independent use.

The later first-year curriculum was broad and compulsory. It comprised
Transfiguration, Charms, Potions, History of Magic, Defence Against the Dark
Arts, Astronomy and Herbology, with flying taught in addition. These were not
seven versions of the same accomplishment. Some dealt with the disciplined
alteration of things, some with spells or mixtures, some with living plants,
some with the heavens, and some with bodies of knowledge whose use might be
immediate or remote. The beginning pupil was expected to encounter a magical
world wider than wand-work alone.

The prescribed books give this breadth a more tangible shape. A first-year was
to possess a grade-one book of spells, a history, a work of magical theory, a
beginner's guide to Transfiguration, references on plants, potions and magical
creatures, and a guide to self-protection. A book list is not a complete
syllabus: it does not tell us which chapters were read, how closely a professor
followed them, or whether the same title survived from one generation to the
next. It does show what Hogwarts intended a beginner to have within reach.

The equipment list makes the same point by other means. Scales and phials
belonged to measurement; a telescope to observation; gloves to the safe
handling of what could bite, burn or poison; the wand and cauldron to arts that
could not be learned by reading alone. Compulsory flying beside a ban on owning
a broom is especially revealing. Hogwarts might require a practical skill
while still controlling the conditions under which a novice practised it. That
is an institutional implication of the later list, not a recovered statement
of educational philosophy.

The common foundation did not remain the whole curriculum. At the end of
second year, later pupils were required to choose at least two additional
subjects from Arithmancy, Muggle Studies, Divination, the Study of Ancient
Runes and Care of Magical Creatures. The choice was made before third year,
not upon entry. Hogwarts therefore postponed this first formal branching until
pupils had spent two years within the common programme.

Nor did choosing an elective mean immediately abandoning the older subjects.
The observed second-years understood that their existing course continued
while new studies were added. Their advice to one another was uneven, but it
connected choice with future work, personal aptitude and the kind of magical
problems a pupil hoped eventually to address. This was specialisation by
addition. It enlarged the course before later examinations narrowed it.

The final two years could be narrower and, on occasion, more specialised. A
later account says that subjects such as Alchemy might be offered if enough
pupils wanted them. The surviving sixth-year schedules, meanwhile, were built
from the subjects in which each pupil had qualified to continue. There was no
single advanced programme taken by all. What a pupil studied near the end of
seven years depended upon previous results, available teaching and, for some
offerings, sufficient demand.

Within that structure, teaching varied with subject and professor. A surviving
first-year Charms lesson began with movement and pronunciation before pupils
attempted to raise feathers from their desks. Success was visible; error could
be heard in a misplaced syllable or seen in an ineffectual wand movement. By
fifth year, another Charms class still relied on individual practice, but the
teacher corrected technique and required additional work when a pupil could
not silence the animal before him. The two lessons suggest continuity in
practice and correction, not a complete philosophy of Charms.

Herbology required a different conjunction of knowledge and action. Before a
second-year class repotted Mandrakes, Professor Sprout asked what they were
used for and why they were dangerous. She then demonstrated the work, required
earmuffs to be fitted securely and warned that even young plants could render
a pupil unconscious. Only after explanation and demonstration did groups begin
handling the plants in the greenhouse. The facility was not merely a room in
which the subject happened to meet. Its living contents, protective equipment
and controlled dangers formed part of the instruction.

Defence Against the Dark Arts could join explanation and encounter even more
closely. In one third-year lesson, Lupin first explained the nature of a
boggart, the difficulty of facing one alone, and the spell by which it might be
repelled. Pupils then practised against the creature itself under supervision.
The lesson ended with points for answers and performance and with instructions
to read and summarise the relevant chapter. Theory, practical magic, immediate
feedback and written consolidation belonged to a single piece of teaching.

Not every subject made such immediate use of wand or greenhouse. Binns taught
fifth-year History of Magic by sustained lecture while pupils took notes, and
he followed a lesson on the giant wars with an extended essay. The pupils'
opinion of his delivery was famously unkind; it is also beside the institutional
point. The classroom still required listening, written record and historical
argument. Hogwarts education included forms of study in which mastery was
expressed on parchment rather than by the successful transformation of the
thing in front of the pupil.

The balance between theory and practice was capable of deliberate alteration.
In 1995 Dolores Umbridge ordered wands put away, prescribed *Defensive Magical
Theory* and announced a carefully structured, Ministry-approved course. Its
stated aims concerned principles, legal recognition and theoretical context;
practical defensive spell-work was refused. This was not the discovery of an
ancient Hogwarts preference. It was a later intervention whose contrast with
earlier practical teaching is precisely what makes it historically useful.

Assessment likewise took more than one form. At the end of first year, later
pupils sat written papers with enchanted anti-cheating quills and were called
individually to perform Charms, Transfiguration and Potions tasks. Third-year
examinations again moved between written knowledge and practical achievement,
from a History paper and midnight Astronomy to working spells, brewing a
potion and keeping a creature safely alive. The exact exercises belonged to
their teachers and year; the repeated division between written and practical
assessment is the stronger institutional pattern.

O.W.L.s have a firmer root before the reconstruction boundary. A Pensieve
memory from the Marauders' school years preserves a Defence Against the Dark
Arts Ordinary Wizarding Level paper in the Great Hall. Professor Flitwick
invigilated; more than a hundred scripts were collected at the end; one
question concerned the signs by which a werewolf might be identified. The
memory gives us an examination, a subject, a setting and a little content. It
does not give us the whole system in that decade.

The later system is more fully recorded. In fifth year, O.W.L.s occupied two successive weeks,
with theory papers in the mornings, practical examinations in the afternoons
and Astronomy examined at night. Papers carried powerful anti-cheating charms,
and forbidden aids were named in advance. The examiners came from outside the
ordinary teaching staff and were associated with the Wizarding Examinations
Authority. One of them, Griselda Marchbanks, remembered examining Dumbledore in
Transfiguration and Charms for his N.E.W.T.s, a recollection that carries the
public qualifications into a much earlier generation without supplying their
original date.

For the written papers, the House tables were removed from the Great Hall and
replaced by individual desks facing the staff-table end. Candidates entered by
class and worked under a large hourglass. Practical examinations were conducted
person by person by the visiting examiners. The later arrangement closely
resembled the hall seen in the older Pensieve memory, but resemblance is not
enough to carry every 1996 rule backwards. What can safely be said is that the
Great Hall had served as an O.W.L. examination room before 1984 and did so
again in the later record.

Results arrived later on individual parchments. The recorded scale divided
passes into Outstanding, Exceeds Expectations and Acceptable, and failures
into Poor, Dreadful and Troll. The humour of the last term should not conceal
the document's practical force. Each subject received its own result, and the
collection of those marks determined which advanced courses remained open.
O.W.L.s were not simply a grander version of the year's ordinary examinations;
they were a gate between the common course and the final stage of schooling.

The approach to that gate was connected with advice about life beyond
Hogwarts. In the later fifth year, pupils were required to attend meetings with
their Heads of House and were given pamphlets on work in healing, banking,
Muggle relations, curse-breaking and Ministry departments. One consultation
made the logic especially plain: an aspiring Auror needed high N.E.W.T. results
and was advised to continue Defence, Transfiguration, Charms and Potions. The
example does not describe every career. It shows the school translating a
future occupation into present subject choices.

<!-- evidence: ootp-ch29-003, ootp-ch29-004 -->

Advice did not itself create eligibility. Before sixth-year schedules were
issued, O.W.L. results were checked against the standards for each chosen
subject. An Acceptable in Transfiguration could be judged insufficient for
N.E.W.T.-level work, while an Exceeds Expectations in Potions became adequate
when Slughorn replaced Snape, who had required Outstanding. The change is a
useful warning against inventing a single Hogwarts-wide threshold. Advanced
entry could depend upon the subject and the professor as well as the public
grade.

Those admitted found that N.E.W.T.-level study demanded more than a smaller
selection of familiar lessons. N.E.W.T.s were described as the highest
qualification Hogwarts offered. By sixth year, non-verbal spells were expected
in Defence, Charms and Transfiguration, while Herbology brought pupils into
contact with more dangerous plants. The surviving account also records a
larger volume of work, but the institutional change matters more here than the
weariness it produced: advanced pupils were asked to perform familiar kinds of
magic under more exacting conditions.

Historical continuity can sometimes be attached to a book more securely than
to a timetable. Newt Scamander states that *Fantastic Beasts and Where to Find
Them* was first published in 1927. Dumbledore's later foreword calls it an
approved Hogwarts textbook from the time of publication and connects it with
Care of Magical Creatures examination results. This does not prove that every
pupil bought every edition or that the subject was taught unchanged. It does
give one named work a dated place in Hogwarts education extending well before
1984.

Other continuities survive as separate points. Galatea Merrythought had taught
Defence Against the Dark Arts for nearly fifty years by the time the young Tom
Riddle sought a post. McGonagall's earlier school record included high O.W.L.
and N.E.W.T. results; the Marauders later sat O.W.L.s; Marchbanks remembered
Dumbledore's N.E.W.T.s. Together these records place subjects and qualifications
in several generations. They do not tell us that the content, standards or
administration between those generations remained the same.

The curriculum could also be reconsidered. Before appointing Sybill Trelawney,
Dumbledore later recalled that he had been disinclined to let Divination
continue at all. The subject did continue, so this is not a history of its
abolition; it is evidence that a place in the curriculum was not beyond review.
The conditional offering of specialist subjects points in the same direction,
while Umbridge's later Defence course shows a more abrupt alteration imposed
upon method. These examples are too scattered to form a smooth history of
reform. They are sufficient to prevent us from mistaking the curriculum of any
one year for an ancient and immovable whole.

The education revealed by these records was therefore more than seven years of
increasingly difficult spells. It began, in the later documented form, with a
wide common foundation; admitted choice gradually; joined reading and written
argument to observation, measurement and dangerous practical work; and made
examinations increasingly consequential as pupils approached adulthood. The
record supports no formal declaration that Hogwarts meant to produce a
particular kind of witch or wizard. Its requirements nevertheless show a
school trying to turn uncontrolled ability into knowledge, disciplined skill
and, eventually, specialised competence.

Such a structure can be described in lists, lessons and examinations. It was
not experienced that way. A pupil encountered it as mornings and nights,
success and embarrassment, books carried, work postponed, friendships formed
and rules endured within a castle that was also a home for most of the year.
The distinction matters. Hogwarts offered an education through institutions;
its pupils had to live through that education one day at a time.
