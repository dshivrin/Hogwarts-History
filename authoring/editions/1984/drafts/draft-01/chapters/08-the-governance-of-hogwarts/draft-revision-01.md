# The Governance of Hogwarts

The reach of Hogwarts may be measured in rules as readily as in walls. During
Armando Dippet's headmastership, a Christmas pantomime in the Great Hall ended
with the hospital wing full and the Hall itself smelling of smoke for months.
Dippet's response was not to improve the scenery or demand a safer second
performance. He prohibited future pantomimes altogether. The episode is comic
at a distance, as unsuccessful school entertainments often are, but its
institutional meaning is plain: the headmaster could turn a single calamity
into a rule for the whole school.

The same production brought Professor Silvanus Kettleburn yet another period
of probation. He accumulated at least sixty-two of them during his employment,
and his relations with Dippet were correspondingly strained. Probation was a
different instrument from prohibition: it subjected an individual member of
staff to continuing judgement without removing him from his post. Hogwarts
could therefore govern by degrees. A headmaster might forbid an activity,
discipline a teacher, or tolerate a hazardous colleague under restriction. The
surviving account does not explain how probation was administered, but it shows
that dismissal was not the only means of staff control.

Control of the staff began with admission to it. When Tom Riddle left Hogwarts
with exceptional academic distinctions, he asked Dippet for a teaching post.
Dippet refused because Riddle was only eighteen, though he invited him to apply
again after gaining further experience. The decision was more discriminating
than a simple rejection. A headmaster could judge not merely a candidate's
attainments but his present suitability, and could close the school door
without pretending that it must remain closed for ever.

Riddle did return, no longer using his old name and with followers waiting
nearby. By then Dumbledore was headmaster. He too refused the application. The
two decisions were made by different men, at different stages of the
candidate's life and for reasons that should not be collapsed into one. What
they have in common is narrower and more useful: on both occasions the
headmaster decided whether an applicant would be given access to Hogwarts as a
teacher. Nothing in these encounters proves that no governor or other authority
could ever participate in an appointment, but the headmaster's judgement was
not ceremonial.

Appointment was only one way of deciding who might remain within the
institution. Rubeus Hagrid was expelled in his third year and his wand was
snapped, yet Dumbledore secured his continued place at Hogwarts as gamekeeper.
The distinction matters. The evidence does not show that Dumbledore cancelled
the expulsion or restored Hagrid's right to perform magic. Instead, he found a
place within the school for someone upon whom severe penalties had already
fallen. Governance could preserve institutional service without pretending
that an earlier judgement had not been made.

A later incident confirms the breadth of this practical discretion. In 1995,
after hostile publicity and complaints from parents, Hagrid attempted to resign
from the teaching staff. Dumbledore refused the resignation, set supportive
letters beside the complaints, and required him to return to his duties. This
does not establish a general law by which a headmaster could compel every
teacher to remain. It does show that public complaint did not of itself govern
the staff, and that the headmaster could treat continuity as an institutional
decision rather than a contest of howlers.

Nor did every decision descend from the headmaster personally. A case from
1992 shows the work divided. After two pupils arrived in a flying car,
Dumbledore warned that any repetition would lead to expulsion and undertook to
write to their families. The immediate punishment, however, belonged to
Professor McGonagall because the boys were members of her House. She imposed
detention and, because the school term had not yet begun, deducted no House
points. Headmaster and Head of House dealt with different parts of the same
offence.

Such division is easily obscured by the prominence of the headmaster's office.
The headmaster spoke for Hogwarts in grave matters and could set the limits of
a subordinate decision, but ordinary authority also travelled through the
staff. A Head of House did not merely offer advice: in the observed case, the
office carried responsibility for punishment. Hogwarts was therefore not
governed simply by a powerful personality at its summit. Its rules acquired
effect through lesser offices whose competence could be practical, immediate
and quite real.

Above or around the headmaster stood the school governors, though their powers
are most clearly recorded only after 1984. In 1993 Lucius Malfoy arrived with
an Order of Suspension bearing the signatures of all twelve governors.
Cornelius Fudge accepted that appointing or suspending a headmaster was their
affair. The scene establishes a board of twelve and a formal-looking means by
which it could remove the head from office. It does not establish when the
board was created, whether twelve had always been its number, or how the
governors themselves were chosen.

<!-- source-access: This is a 1993 event and cannot be silently backdated. The signed order and Fudge's response establish the governor mechanism in that crisis, not its origin or complete procedure. -->

The signatures did not make the decision sound. Dumbledore later reported that
several governors had been threatened, and the other eleven recalled him when
the crisis worsened. By the end of the year Malfoy himself had been removed as
a governor. Formal capacity and legitimate exercise must therefore be kept
apart. The board could suspend and recall a headmaster and could cease to
include one of its own members; it could also be manipulated by a governor able
to frighten his colleagues. Hogwarts possessed oversight, but oversight did
not guarantee independence or wisdom.

The governors' concern extended beyond the person of the headmaster. After
Dumbledore's death in 1997, Professor McGonagall consulted the Heads of Houses
about whether Hogwarts should reopen. Their discussion ranged from the wishes
of pupils to the likelihood that parents would keep children at home. Yet
Professor Flitwick observed that the governors must be consulted because the
final decision was theirs. Senior staff could deliberate about the school's
future; the board was understood to decide whether the institution would
continue to receive pupils at all.

The Ministry of Magic stood at another boundary. During the Chamber crisis of
the 1940s, Dippet refused Riddle permission to remain at school for the summer,
judging even an orphanage safer than Hogwarts while attacks continued. He also
said that the Ministry was discussing closure. The headmaster controlled the
immediate decision about residence, but the possible survival of the school
had drawn an outside authority into the matter. Discussion is not a decree,
and the episode supplies no statute; it nevertheless shows that a mortal danger
within Hogwarts could become more than an internal school question.

Later conflicts show why the relationship cannot be reduced to either school
independence or Ministry command. In 1994 Dumbledore believed that Peter
Pettigrew was alive and that Sirius Black was innocent, but he had no proof
capable of overturning the accepted account. He stated plainly that he could
not overrule the Minister of Magic. Here the decisive matter was an external
criminal judgement, not the management of a lesson, House or member of staff.
The headmaster's authority was formidable inside Hogwarts, but belief and
office together did not make him a court of appeal for the wizarding state.

The balance could also be negotiated. In 1995 the Ministry initially expelled
Harry Potter for underage magic and announced that his wand would be destroyed.
After discussions with Dumbledore, it revised the decision: Potter was
suspended pending a disciplinary hearing, and the question of his wand was
deferred. The headmaster did not simply nullify the Ministry's action, but
neither was the first notice beyond challenge. Hogwarts leadership could
intervene when an external sanction determined whether a pupil might remain at
school.

Fudge himself supplied an unusually candid description of the preceding
arrangement. During his break with Dumbledore in 1995, he complained that the
headmaster had been given free rein: he had hired a werewolf, retained Hagrid,
and decided what pupils should be taught without referring each matter to the
Ministry. The complaint came from an angry Minister threatening closer
scrutiny, and it should not be mistaken for a neutral charter. Precisely for
that reason, it reveals the political character of Hogwarts autonomy. Wide
latitude could exist in practice until confidence failed, whereupon yesterday's
discretion became today's grievance.

The following school year turned that grievance into machinery. Educational
Decree Twenty-two allowed the Ministry to appoint a teacher if the headmaster
could not find one; Decree Twenty-three created the office of Hogwarts High
Inquisitor and empowered its holder to inspect the teaching staff. The public
announcement described this as an unprecedented degree of Ministry control.
Whatever claims accompanied the reform, its institutional novelty is the
essential fact. Powers that had previously remained outside ordinary school
administration were deliberately carried into it.

The intervention soon passed from inspecting authority to command over
discipline. Educational Decree Twenty-five gave the High Inquisitor power to
alter punishments imposed by other members of staff and to remove pupil
privileges. Umbridge immediately used it to displace McGonagall's decision in a
Quidditch dispute. The contrast with the flying-car case is exact enough to be
instructive. In 1992 a Head of House exercised responsibility within the
school's existing order; three years later a newly created office was empowered
to override such decisions. The latter was a change to the machinery, not an
example of its timeless operation.

Even this enlarged authority was not indivisible. When Umbridge dismissed
Sybill Trelawney under Decree Twenty-three, she also attempted to expel her from
the castle. Dumbledore accepted the dismissal but denied that the second power
followed from the first: permission to remain at Hogwarts still belonged to
the headmaster. The result was a peculiar division in which a teacher could be
removed from her post by one authority and sheltered within the institution by
another. Crisis government did not replace every older power at once; it
produced overlapping commands inside the same doorway.

The same evening exposed the limit written into the Ministry's appointment
power. Umbridge expected to fill the vacant Divination post, but Dumbledore had
already appointed Firenze. Decree Twenty-two authorised Ministry appointment
only if the headmaster was unable to find a candidate; Dumbledore's choice
therefore prevented the condition from arising. It would be misleading to turn
this contest into evidence that staffing had always been shared in precisely
that way. The decree created the contest. What the incident demonstrates is
that even an intervention designed to narrow the headmaster's discretion had to
define—and thereby acknowledge—the discretion that remained.

The Umbridge administration is thus most useful as evidence of change. It
should not be mined for supposed ancient customs merely because its decrees
operated inside an ancient school. Inspection, dismissal, appointment and
punishment were redistributed by measures announced as new, and their effects
were observed in particular confrontations. They reveal what political power
could do to Hogwarts when it entered the institution directly. They do not
show that the same apparatus had governed Hogwarts before the intervention.

No surviving account arranges these offices into a complete constitution.
What emerges instead is a working distribution of power. The headmaster could
make rules, judge appointments and direct the staff; Heads of House exercised
authority within their charge; governors supervised the headmaster and could
decide whether the school remained open; the Ministry acted where external law
or public danger reached Hogwarts, and could enlarge its part by legislation.
The boundaries were not always clean. Indeed, they are clearest when a
signature was coerced, a decision challenged, or two officers claimed the same
threshold.

Hogwarts was therefore neither the private kingdom of its headmaster nor a
mere department of the Ministry. It was a school whose government depended
upon offices strong enough to act and limited enough to collide. That
arrangement could protect continuity, as when a servant was retained or a
coerced suspension reversed; it could also admit pressure, delay and abuse.
Its historical importance lies less in a tidy diagram of rank than in the
conditions it created for the life of the school. Decisions about authority
became decisions about who might teach, what rules could be enforced, and what
kind of education Hogwarts was permitted to offer.
