# Chapter 14 — Quidditch

Quidditch made House identity visible at a distance. A pupil might belong to a
House in the dormitory, at table and in the award of points, but on the pitch
that belonging acquired colours, players, opponents and a result. In the
later school, the four communities did not merely keep teams. They gathered
to watch them, dressed the ground in their loyalties and carried victory or
defeat back into the castle. Quidditch gave an existing division of the school
a season of public tests.

The conversion depended upon more than the fourteen pupils in the air. A
House team represented companions who never mounted a broom; a captain's
decision could become a subject of common expectation; points scored in one
match could shape the meaning of another months later. Referee, commentator,
staff and spectators turned play into an occasion witnessed by the
institution. The history required here is therefore not principally a history
of a ball game. It is the history of the arrangements by which Hogwarts made
that game its own.

That history cannot begin with the founders playing the game now known to
their successors. Hogwarts was founded more than a thousand years before the
early 1990s, though its exact date remains uncertain. European broom flight
was certainly known in the tenth century, but the surviving account describes
an uncomfortable and ill-controlled means of travel. The earliest record of
the game at Queerditch Marsh belongs to the eleventh century and already
presupposes improvements in turning, speed and height. It is evidence for an
early broom game, not for Quidditch at Hogwarts.

The game itself was assembled by stages. A twelfth-century description shows
practised teams, three goals at either end and recognisable ancestors of the
Chaser, Beater and Bludger. The pursuit of a Golden Snidget entered play only
after Barberus Bragge released one at a match in 1269; the reward he offered
left its mark upon the later value of the catch. Protection of the bird came
later still, and Bowman Wright's mechanical Golden Snitch replaced a creature
that the earliest game had never contained. One of the most distinctive
features of modern Quidditch thus arrived centuries after Hogwarts had begun.

Other familiar features are younger again. Permanent-pitch guidance is
recorded by 1398; scoring areas by 1620; nationally agreed rules in 1750; and
fixed hoops only in 1883. Sporting brooms continued to change in the
nineteenth and twentieth centuries, from the Cushioning Charm to
purpose-built racing models and the later Nimbus designs. These dates are not
a substitute for Hogwarts history. They establish the narrower and more
important point that the school could not have received modern Quidditch all
at once, because modern Quidditch did not yet exist.

The earliest recoverable traces at Hogwarts come long after this uncertain
beginning. No surviving record dates the first school match, the
formation of the four House teams, the first award of the Quidditch Cup or the
construction of the pitch. The chronology therefore has a conspicuous open
space. Hogwarts predates mature Quidditch; by the later twentieth century the
game was established at the school; the evidence does not show the
transformation between those conditions.

One trace is the pitch itself. A later account of Gilderoy Lockhart's school
years records that he received a week's detention for carving his name into
the Quidditch pitch. The incident is more informative about Lockhart than
about sport, but it fixes the ground as a recognised and named part of
Hogwarts by then. It does not say when the pitch was laid out, what facilities
then surrounded it or whether it had already assumed every form later
observed.

Another trace survives in a Slytherin team photograph. Regulus Black appears
in it as Seeker, seated among House players in their sporting identity. The
photograph is modest evidence, but firm: by Regulus's school years at least
one House possessed a named team and a specialised player. It gives no match
result, complete roster or Cup record. Nor does one preserved team photograph
prove how long the arrangement had existed before the shutter closed.

By the period in which school Quidditch can be closely observed, all four
Houses fielded teams. Their contests used the familiar seven-player
arrangement: three Chasers, two Beaters, a Keeper and a Seeker. Gryffindor,
Slytherin, Hufflepuff and Ravenclaw appear not as temporary sides assembled
for a single afternoon but as recurring representatives of permanent school
communities. That correspondence gave the competition its stability. Pupils
changed; the House name did not.

The positions mattered institutionally because they divided responsibility
within a House's public representative body. Chasers accumulated goals;
Beaters managed the danger of the Bludgers; the Keeper defended the hoops;
and the Seeker pursued the catch that could overturn the arithmetic of a
match. A team could therefore possess both a collective reputation and
recognisable individual offices. Those offices, rather than the finer laws
governing them, made the team socially legible within the school.

At their head stood the captain. Later evidence shows captains summoning team
meetings, arranging practice, directing tactics, assessing candidates and
choosing replacements. The badge attached to the office gave it a status
readily compared with other recognised pupil distinctions. Captaincy did not
remove staff authority, but it entrusted a considerable portion of the team's
daily government to a pupil whose decisions were visible to the House.

Selection likewise appears most clearly in later practice. Open trials for a
Keeper were held in 1995, and the following year a captain received names,
scheduled trials and tested candidates in the skills of their proposed
positions. Heads of House could direct interested pupils towards the process,
but the captain made the sporting judgement. Even then the evidence describes
particular Houses and seasons. It does not establish that every House always
held open trials, at the same time, by the same tests.

Once selected, a team demanded time. Three evenings of practice could be
scheduled early in a season; five sessions in a week, daily practice and
near-daily training appear when Cup prospects tightened. A captain could use
drills, tactical rehearsals and previous trial performances to keep the side
functioning when illness or injury removed a player. These schedules show the
weight pupils and captains placed upon the competition, not a compulsory
training rule imposed equally upon every team.

The broom beneath a player introduced another distinction. First-years in
1991 were told not to bring personal broomsticks, while their flying lessons
used school brooms. Harry Potter's appointment to the Gryffindor team in that
year was treated as exceptional and followed staff consultation. It would be
unsafe to turn either fact into an absolute historical ban on first-years
playing. The documented rule concerned what they might bring; the documented
selection concerned an exception made for unusual talent.

Nor did all selected players take the air on equivalent equipment. School
brooms were available at later trials, yet personal ownership could provide a
marked advantage, and in 1992 Lucius Malfoy supplied the entire Slytherin team
with Nimbus Two Thousand and Ones. The gift changed the material balance
without changing the House structure of the contest. Quidditch could present
itself as common school competition while allowing differences of wealth,
patronage and technology to enter the air with the players.

The pitch gave these arrangements a permanent place. Later observers found a
field bordered by raised stands, with changing accommodation for the teams.
Equipment could be kept under Madam Hooch's care, and by 1995 a captain used
an office beside the changing room as well as a crate of match balls and
training apparatus. These features need not have been built together. What
they supplied in the observed institution was a separation between
preparation, play, supervision and spectatorship within one sporting ground.

The same ground could be diverted to another purpose. During the Triwizard
year, when the Inter-House Quidditch Cup had been cancelled, the pitch was
transformed into the maze for the Tournament's final task. Its stands still
served an assembled school audience, even though the centre no longer held a
match. The episode does not reveal the pitch's original design. It does show
how thoroughly Hogwarts had already equipped one part of its grounds for
collective attention.

On an ordinary match day the ground received the institution it had come to
serve. In 1991 the whole school crowded into the raised stands. Later
matches again drew pupils from the Houses and staff from the castle, while the
teams changed below before emerging in House colours. The passage from room
to field made players into representatives before a public already arranged
to recognise them as such.

Supporters made the representation unmistakable. Scarves, banners, badges and
an enchanted hat displayed allegiance; applause and coordinated songs gave it
a voice. The most notorious song in the surviving matches was designed to
unsettle a player rather than merely praise a side, and the same season ended
with the victorious House appropriating it for celebration. Quidditch did not
invent inter-House hostility, but it gave loyalty and hostility a scheduled
place in which to be performed before the school.

The match itself opened through recognisable acts. Captains shook hands;
Madam Hooch released or set the balls in play and sounded the beginning; a
student commentator described the action through magical amplification. The
referee awarded penalties and addressed fouls, while a member of staff checked
commentary that wandered too far from the game. These arrangements connected
the laws of play to school authority without removing the occasion from pupil
life.

Commentary was itself a small school office rather than an impersonal voice.
Lee Jordan brought loyalties and argument to the microphone; later pupils
performed the task differently, and candidates could be directed to their
Heads of House at the beginning of term. The changing voices mattered because
they interpreted a common sight for a common audience. Supervision restrained
them, but did not erase personality from the school account of its own match.

Spectatorship did not always follow a simple fourfold line. In a 1992 match,
pupils outside the two competing Houses aligned themselves with one side or
the other. At a Cup decider, the tension reached corridors and classrooms
before the teams entered the ground. A House match could therefore draw the
whole school into temporary coalitions as well as inherited loyalties. Seven
players represented one House, but the result rearranged attention beyond it.

A match acquired further weight from its place in a season. By November 1991
the Quidditch season was under way; in later years practices and fixtures
continued across terms. Pitch access had to be arranged, and the condition of
the standings could make one House train more frequently as another result
altered its prospects. The school calendar therefore converted a succession
of games into expectation: what had happened already changed what the next
match required.

The Quidditch Cup was determined by more than a count of victories. Teams
calculated the margin by which they had to win, and scores accumulated in ways
that could leave a House victorious in a match yet short of the Cup. Later
final fixtures resolved those calculations and placed the trophy in the hands
of the winning side. The surviving accounts never pause to give a complete
tournament code. They establish instead that goals, margins and earlier
matches remained alive in the season's final arithmetic.

Victory then escaped the ground. In 1994 pupils surged onto the pitch around
the Cup; in 1996 players and trophy were carried into the castle; in 1997 a
common-room celebration received the news of the deciding score. Such scenes
made achievement collective without making every participant a player. The
House possessed the victory socially, even though a small team had won it in
the air.

Defeat was collective too. Captains trained against the memory of previous
results; pupils anticipated the humiliation or prestige attached to the next
one; players carried individual errors into a House's public account. This
pressure helps explain both the honour attached to team membership and the
severity with which selection and practice could be regarded. A player acted
as an individual, but did so beneath colours whose reputation preceded and
outlasted any one school career.

The game also required Hogwarts to decide how much danger could remain part of
school sport. Bludgers were dangerous by design; Beaters existed to direct
them, and fouls and penalties assumed that physical interference would occur.
Later teams practised and played through cold, rain and violent wind. This was
not risk accidentally intruding upon an otherwise gentle activity. A measure
of risk belonged to the game Hogwarts organised.

Organisation did not mean the absence of limits. Madam Hooch refereed,
awarded penalties and kept the balls secured; captains could call for time;
staff could intervene when danger ceased to be ordinary play. Training took
place under supervision when external threats required it. The school thus
accepted a hazardous sport while surrounding it with custody, judgement and
the capacity to respond.

Injury remained frequent enough to shape the institution around the pitch. A
rogue Bludger broke Harry Potter's arm in 1992; other practices and matches
produced collisions and serious hospital-wing consequences. Play and training
did not become harmless because treatment was available, but medical care
made the consequences part of school provision rather than a private burden
upon the injured pupil. The evidence shows tolerance of considerable danger,
not an absence of response to it.

Some circumstances exceeded even that broad tolerance. In 1993 Dementors
entered the vicinity of a match, Harry fell from his broom and Dumbledore
arrested the fall before the hospital wing received him. The episode cannot
stand for ordinary Quidditch danger: the threat came from outside the sport.
Its value is to show the distinction between hazards accepted within play and
a breach that required extraordinary protection.

Emergency could stop play altogether. During the attacks of 1993, a scheduled
match was cancelled at the pitch and pupils were returned under school orders.
This was not a revision of Quidditch but a suspension of it before a more
urgent danger. The cancellation is historically useful because there had been
a fixture to cancel, teams ready to play and spectators expecting an
afternoon whose ordinary sequence was suddenly denied them.

The following exceptional interruption was announced for an entire year. In
1994 the Inter-House Quidditch Cup was cancelled because Hogwarts was hosting
the Triwizard Tournament; the pitch was later turned into the maze. Removing
Quidditch required explicit notice because it removed one of the expected
structures of the school calendar. When the Cup resumed in the next school
year, practice and pitch booking resumed with it.

Administrative interference could disrupt a season from within. Under
Dolores Umbridge, players were banned and brooms confiscated after a match;
Gryffindor consequently had to replace a Seeker and two Beaters. The wider
constitutional significance of those powers lies elsewhere. For Quidditch,
the episode shows a team attempting to preserve its place in the competition
after authority had removed much of its playing strength. The institution
continued, but not untouched.

Quidditch accumulated memory because each year's players entered positions
already held by others. Regulus Black's team photograph preserved one such
identity. Charlie Weasley remained a measure of Gryffindor expectation after
he had left; Cedric Diggory was remembered not only as a pupil but as
Hufflepuff's captain and Seeker. A captain's badge, a recalled result or an old
team image allowed a brief school career to persist as part of a House's
account of itself.

The surviving record is nevertheless fragmentary. It offers photographs,
remembered matches, changing captains and later Cup celebrations rather than
an unbroken register of teams. Institutional memory can be strong without
being complete: expectations pass from one team to another even when the
archive does not disclose the first team or preserve every name between.

Hogwarts did not create Quidditch, and the evidence does not reveal when the
school first adopted it. What can be recovered is the fitness of the mature
institution to the school that received it. Four Houses supplied continuing
names and loyalties; the pitch supplied a common arena; captains and teams
supplied achievement; the year supplied recurrence; and the Cup made one
match answer to those before it. Spectatorship completed the arrangement by
making each House's triumph and failure visible to all four.

By the period in which its workings can be closely observed, players and
captains could change, brooms could confer new advantages and
extraordinary circumstances could cancel a match or a season without
displacing Quidditch's institutional role. It remained the recurring public
contest in which House identity took teams, scores, witnesses and memory. Yet
the game that Hogwarts made local was never Hogwarts's alone: its balls,
positions and ambitions belonged to a sporting culture organised across
Britain and far beyond it. From the edge of the school pitch, the history of
Hogwarts therefore begins to turn towards the wider wizarding world of which
the school was only one institution.
