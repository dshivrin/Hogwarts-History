## Purpose

Examine what changed, and what did not, as Hogwarts entered the modern
wizarding world. The chapter treats Hogwarts as an institution rather than as
a sequence of famous lives. It asks how an ancient residential school operated
within twentieth-century systems of admission, public examination, government,
communication, war and material technology, without presuming that those
systems formed a planned programme of reform.

## Central historical question

What changed about Hogwarts as it entered the modern age, and what remained
recognisably Hogwarts despite those changes?

## Hypothesis to test

The proposed hypothesis was that Hogwarts modernised by layering new systems
over old ones rather than replacing the old institution wholesale. The
evidence review qualifies that proposition in three ways:

1. Several of the clearest material and national layers—plumbing, coordinated
   transport and the concealed London platform—were in place before the
   twentieth century and are already owned by Chapters 6 and 15.
2. Much of the twentieth-century evidence demonstrates the operation or
   persistence of administrative machinery, not its date of origin.
3. The best documented twentieth-century changes are discrete responses to
   particular needs or pressures, not parts of a recoverable modernisation
   programme.

The working proposition for the outline is therefore narrower: **modern
Hogwarts was an old school operating through inherited national systems,
regular administrative practices and case-specific adaptations, while crisis,
war and technological change made its relationship with the wider world more
consequential.** This remains a bounded synthesis, not a claim of steady
progress.

## Chronological and source-access boundary

The reconstructed narrative remains bounded to 1984. The principal window is
the twentieth century through 1984. Earlier evidence appears only to establish
what the twentieth-century school inherited.

The evidence is divided into five access classes:

1. reconstructed pre-1984 knowledge or explicit original-book core;
2. pre-1984 events carried only by later testimony;
3. post-1984 observation of an older institution, without an origin claim;
4. later editorial history;
5. excluded or deferred material.

No 1991-or-later practice may be treated as pre-1984 merely because it looks
established. The presentation model is a unified narrative whose hidden
evidence comments preserve event date, source date and inference boundary.

## Required analytical distinctions

- change in Hogwarts versus change in wider wizarding society;
- a practice's existence versus the date of its origin;
- inherited infrastructure versus twentieth-century innovation;
- administrative regularity versus a planned bureaucracy;
- adaptation to one pupil or crisis versus a general policy;
- absence of Muggle technology versus ideological rejection of it;
- wartime reputation versus documented wartime measures;
- a Headmaster's action versus an institutional consequence for the school.

## Supported chapter subjects

- The nineteenth-century transport and material systems inherited by the
  twentieth-century school, used only as a compact starting point.
- Twentieth-century contact with Muggle-raised or unsupported prospective
  pupils, including personal explanation, formal conduct expectations,
  financial help, supplies and travel instructions.
- Evidence that offices, records and public examinations operated across
  several twentieth-century generations, while their beginnings remain
  unknown.
- The 1943 Chamber crisis as evidence that a school emergency could acquire
  Ministry, closure, disciplinary and public consequences.
- The First Wizarding War only insofar as later participant testimony describes
  Hogwarts as a comparatively safe place and Dumbledore as a deterrent.
- Hogwarts's magical environment as a material limit on electrical and similar
  Muggle technologies, contrasted cautiously with selective technological
  adoption in wider wizarding Britain.
- A conclusion separating documented continuity, discrete adaptation and
  unsupported narratives of progress.

## Relationship to Chapters 9, 10, 15, 17, 18 and 20

- **Chapter 9** owns the educational system: admission mechanics, subject
  structure, teaching, examinations, qualifications and career preparation.
  Chapter 19 may use a small number of twentieth-century examples only to ask
  what sort of national institution could reach children outside established
  wizarding households and preserve comparable offices across generations.
- **Chapter 10** owns ordinary pupil experience, correspondence, prefect work,
  discipline and school routine. Chapter 19 does not reconstruct daily life.
- **Chapter 15** owns Hogwarts's managed boundary with the wider world,
  including the full transport history, Hogsmeade threshold, owl post,
  commerce, government, governors and external examinations. Chapter 19 uses
  the pre-twentieth-century transport settlement only as inherited context and
  does not retell it.
- **Chapter 17** owns dangers, defensive layers, containment and institutional
  response to disaster. Chapter 19 uses the 1943 crisis only to show that a
  modern school emergency could become a matter of public authority and
  institutional survival.
- **Chapter 18** owns headship as an office and the decisions of Dippet and
  Dumbledore. Chapter 19 excludes the Lupin and Hagrid cases from its advancing
  argument because Chapter 18 already uses them to show the school being
  altered around exceptional inclusion.
- **Chapter 20** owns the biographical and institutional transition from
  Dippet to Dumbledore. Chapter 19 may name either only where necessary to
  identify the school action or source, never to narrate the succession.

## Editorial notes

- Prefer the dateable twentieth-century case to a catalogue of familiar modern
  Hogwarts details.
- If evidence shows only continuity, say continuity; do not rename it reform.
- Use later observation chiefly to mark an origin gap or a mature form, not to
  populate the 1984 reconstruction.
- Every section must answer the chapter's change-and-continuity question rather
  than merely collect twentieth-century facts.
- Keep visible prose readable; place the technical source-access limits in
  adjacent comments during drafting.
