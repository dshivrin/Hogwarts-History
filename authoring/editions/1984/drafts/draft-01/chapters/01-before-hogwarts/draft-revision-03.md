# Chapter One

## Before Hogwarts

### Before the School

The history of Hogwarts begins before Hogwarts had a history of its own. A
school preserves registers, rules, appointments, disputes and recollections;
the world preceding it leaves no such convenient deposit. What survives from
that world is scattered among later histories, old objects, specialist books
and the imperfect notice of Muggles. The fragments are unequal, but they are
not silent. They describe a magical society already in possession of skilled
arts, already sharing Britain with its non-magical neighbours, and already
facing the difficulty of remaining visible without becoming vulnerable.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002, qtta-ch01-002, ext-a41-001, fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002, ext-a45-001, cos-ch09-003 -->

Hogwarts is older than the present millennium. Its precise year of foundation
was uncertain even in the account most commonly taught at the school, and no
day or season can responsibly be supplied. The secure outline is spare. Four
witches and wizards built a castle far from Muggle observation, sought out
young people who showed signs of magic, brought them to that place and educated
them there. The founders, the gathering of pupils, the remoteness of the site
and the purpose of instruction belong to history; much of the picturesque
detail later attached to them belongs elsewhere.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

That outline corrects one persistent illusion. Hogwarts did not bring magic
into an empty country. The people who made the school were already accomplished
practitioners, and the children they sought displayed magic before the school
received them. Knowledge, skill and magical identity therefore existed on both
sides of the foundation. What changed was not the existence of magic but the
way part of magical society attempted to preserve and transmit it.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Schools tend to annex the past in retrospect. Once generations have learnt
Potions, Charms, Ancient Runes or Herbology under those names, the subjects take
on the appearance of timeless divisions. They were not. A useful plant did not
wait for a Herbology lesson before revealing its virtues, nor did an ancient
maker need a timetable before carving runes. The school subject is an
arrangement of knowledge, not proof of the knowledge's beginning.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002, ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002, ext-a42-001 -->

No general system of education before Hogwarts can now be described. There is
no evidence of an earlier British school, a common curriculum or an ordinary
course of childhood instruction. That forbids an invented procession of
village masters and family academies; it does not require us to imagine that
each generation rediscovered magic afresh. A society capable of producing the
founders had plainly learnt how to carry difficult knowledge across time, even
if most of the routes have disappeared.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

### Old Arts

Among the oldest things at Hogwarts is an object that was old before the school
existed. The Hogwarts Pensieve is made of ornately carved stone and engraved
with modified Saxon runes. It is older than the castle that now contains it.
The runes belong to the workmanship of an ancient magical artefact; the
surviving account does not tell us whether the inscription itself performed any
magic.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002 -->

The Pensieve acquired a story equal to its age. One tradition says that the
founders found it half-buried on the very spot where they chose to build. The
source calls the legend unsubstantiated, and it should remain so. The object is
older than Hogwarts; the story of how it came there is not thereby proved. What
survives is still remarkable enough: before the school, a maker had selected
and carved the stone, engraved its modified runes and fashioned an object that
endured into the institution that followed.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002, ext-a17-003 -->

Another old craft comes to us by way of a less dignified object. A European
manuscript records witches and wizards using flying broomsticks in 962. The
early broom offered one forward speed and the ability to rise, descend and
stop. It was uncomfortable, limited and unlikely to inspire nostalgia in
anyone required to travel far upon it. It nevertheless shows an everyday
magical technology in use and still being improved in the broad age from which
Hogwarts emerged. The uncertain date of the school's foundation prevents us
from saying whether the record falls just before or just after its opening.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002 -->

The broom also joined travel to discretion. Unlike a conspicuous flying beast,
it could be hidden without much difficulty, an advantage recognised long before
secrecy became an international body of law. Timber had to be chosen, worked
and charmed; controls had to answer reliably enough for a rider to trust them.
The manuscript does not name the makers or their teachers, but a repeatable
device already in use is evidence of knowledge passing between hands. Solitary
inspiration may produce a marvel. A working craft requires memory.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002 -->

Wandcraft carries a longer tradition, though not so tidy a chronology. The
Ollivander shop sign claims a family wandmaking history from 382 BC. The
present Mr Ollivander also believes that his ancestors reached Britain with the
Romans and sold crafted wands to British wizards whose own instruments were
crude and unreliable. The shop's date is a public tradition, and the Roman
story a descendant's belief; neither is an unbroken ledger from antiquity.
Both preserve the memory of wandmaking as a craft with a history outside
Hogwarts.

<!-- evidence: ps-ch05-007, ext-a41-001 -->

The medieval record is firmer. Geraint Ollivander was a celebrated wandmaker
whose written observations survived in later family practice. The wood itself
demanded knowledge: only a minority of trees yield timber fit for a wand, and
recognising them takes long experience. Bowtruckles may direct a trained eye
towards a promising tree. Plant lore, creature lore and the maker's judgement
thus met at the workbench before the wand performed any magic at all.

<!-- evidence: ext-a42-001, ext-a42-002 -->

Creature knowledge survives differently. Dragons, griffins, unicorns,
phoenixes and centaurs entered medieval Muggle art and writing, often in forms
that would have dismayed a competent magizoologist. Their appearance records
encounters, reports and memories strong enough to cross from magical nature
into non-magical culture. A creature need not be correctly classified to
become part of the knowledge of an age.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002 -->

These remains do not add up to an early timetable. They show a culture of use.
People carved stone, worked wood and magical materials, flew, carried wands,
improved tools and learnt the habits of dangerous creatures. Hogwarts would
eventually arrange many kinds of knowledge under one roof. It did not create
the world on which that roof was raised.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002, qtta-ch01-002, ps-ch05-007, ext-a41-001, ext-a42-001, ext-a42-002, fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002 -->

### Knowledge in Use

Craft implies teaching in the broadest sense, though not necessarily a
profession of teachers. A maker must learn why one piece of wood answers and
another fails. A rider must learn how to rise and, more urgently, how to stop.
Written observations such as Geraint Ollivander's preserved part of a craft;
tools, demonstrations and failed attempts preserved other parts. Knowledge
could pass through a family, a workshop or a neighbour without producing a
school register.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002, ext-a42-001, ext-a42-002 -->

Learning of this kind did not separate itself neatly from work. Selecting
timber, tending a garden, preparing a remedy or assisting an experienced hand
could all provide instruction without becoming a formal lesson. An old object
might preserve decisions its maker never wrote down; an unsuccessful broom
might teach balance in a particularly memorable fashion. Such means were local
and uneven, but skill could accumulate through them.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002, ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002, ext-a42-001, ext-a42-002 -->

Potioneering belonged to this practical world, but its earliest British history
is obscure. No direct evidence establishes ordinary household potion-making in
tenth-century Britain. Two centuries later, however, Linfred of Stinchcombe
cultivated unusual plants, experimented behind closed doors and supplied
medicinal help to Muggle neighbours, who took him for an eccentric but useful
gardener. His home was at once garden, workshop and local source of healing.

<!-- evidence: ext-a38-001 -->

Wizarding historians credit Linfred with remedies that developed into potions
still known in later centuries, including the ancestors of Skele-gro and
Pepperup Potion. He sold his cures to other witches and wizards as well as
quietly treating his neighbours. His preparations therefore travelled beyond
Stinchcombe, although the account does not reveal whether he wrote down his
methods, demonstrated them or guarded them closely.

<!-- evidence: ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002 -->

Linfred is not a witness to the founders' century. He lived in the twelfth
century, when Hogwarts was already established. Yet that later date makes him
instructive in a different way. The castle had not swallowed every useful
plant, healing practice or new preparation within its walls. A wizard could
work at home, serve a local community and contribute lasting remedies to
magical practice. Hogwarts never possessed a monopoly on useful knowledge.

<!-- evidence: ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002 -->

His garden belongs beside the wandmaker's woodpile. Linfred cultivated unusual
plants for remedies; wandmakers learnt which rare trees yielded good timber and
watched for the Bowtruckles that inhabited them. Gardens, woods and workshops
did not observe the borders later drawn between Herbology, Potions, the study
of creatures and wandcraft. Useful knowledge met where the work required it.

<!-- evidence: ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002, ext-a42-001 -->

Stories carried another kind of knowledge. In later centuries, tales associated
with Beedle became familiar bedtime reading, and one well-known story taught
many wizarding children that magic could not reverse death. No spell was learnt
from that lesson, yet a limit upon magical action was. Beedle cannot be placed
in the homes of the founders' first pupils, but his later reception shows how a
magical culture may pass on judgement and restraint as well as technique.

<!-- evidence: beedle-fm-002, beedle-ch04-001 -->

The weakness of scattered teaching is easy to see. Knowledge held by one
family, craft or gifted healer could travel only as far as its keepers and
circumstances allowed. A child born near accomplished practitioners stood in a
different position from one whose magic appeared where little help was at hand.
The record gives no measure of that inequality, but the founders' decision to
seek children rather than wait for them is intelligible against it.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Hogwarts thus arose from a world in which magical knowledge was real,
consequential and dispersed. Some of it belonged to stone or tools; some to
skilled hands, gardens and written craft lore; some to stories and cautions.
The school created a place in which much of it could be taught repeatedly. The
hands and households that possessed it, however, belonged to the same British
landscape as their Muggle neighbours.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002, qtta-ch01-002, ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002, ext-a42-001, ext-a42-002, beedle-fm-002, beedle-ch04-001, cos-ch09-003 -->

### Among Muggles

Magical and non-magical Britain shared roads, fields, forests, harbours and
settlements. Before the International Statute of Secrecy, they also shared
ordinary ways of getting about. Witches, wizards and Muggles travelled in
horse-drawn carts and sailing ships; in defence, a wizard might use a sword as
well as a wand. These accounts cover a broad pre-Statute past rather than the
founders' particular decade, but they place magical people inside historical
Britain, not beside it in a sealed and parallel country.

<!-- evidence: ext-a08-003, ext-a45-001 -->

Shared transport meant shared practical conditions. A cart followed a road; a
sailing ship depended upon harbours, crews and weather, however much magic
might ease one part of the passage. Magical travellers did not need a separate
system for every ordinary task. They could use the same useful thing as their
neighbours and remain magical while doing so.

<!-- evidence: ext-a45-001 -->

Even the cauldron once belonged to both populations. Muggles and magical people
used the same suspended cooking pots before changing household technologies
left the cauldron chiefly in wizarding hands, still well suited to potion-making
over a naked flame. The source gives no century for this change and cannot
furnish the founders' kitchen. It preserves a smaller history: an ordinary
object could be shared, adapted and eventually come to look peculiarly magical.

<!-- evidence: ext-a40-001, ext-a40-002 -->

The broom belongs to the same mixed landscape. Its rider used magic, but the
object itself was commonplace enough to hide. Discretion worked through
ordinary things: a broom that could be put away, a road travelled without
display, a remote place chosen because repeated magic would be difficult to
explain. Muggle observation was already a practical concern, though the two
populations had not yet been divided by the later law of secrecy.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002, cos-ch09-003 -->

Muggle records show how imperfect that discretion could be. Medieval art and
literature remembered magical creatures while confusing their names and
natures. One writer known as Brother Benedict described a violent encounter in
a herb garden with what he took to be a monstrous ferret. Later magical
interpretation identifies a Jarvey, probably hunting gnomes. A cultivated
garden, a magical creature and a bewildered observer occupied the same patch of
ground; the record kept the incident and misunderstood nearly all of it.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002, fb-ch02-003 -->

Such records show proximity, not easy intimacy. A Muggle might glimpse
something extraordinary without knowing its name or how it fitted into the
larger magical world. Magical people possessed names and techniques that their
neighbours replaced with marvel, monster or mistake. Yet contact was not always
hostile. Linfred's neighbours sought his remedies without recognising their
magical origin. Service, curiosity, error and suspicion could all occur within
the same shared landscape.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002, fb-ch02-003, ext-a38-001 -->

This was the setting in which magical learning acquired a problem of place. A
single act of instruction or experiment might pass unnoticed. The repeated
gathering of magical children, teachers, equipment and practice was harder to
hide. Continuity needed somewhere to live, but a place made continuity visible.

<!-- evidence: ext-a38-001, qtta-ch01-002, ext-a40-001, ext-a45-001, cos-ch09-003 -->

### Fear and Distance

The strongest founding account places Muggle fear and the persecution of
witches and wizards beside the choice of a remote site. The founders built away
from prying eyes. We are given no map of attacks and no exact year in which the
danger peaked. What the account makes plain is that concealment belonged to the
castle's purpose; it was not a picturesque quality supplied by later legend.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Medieval creature history suggests how fear could spread. Reports of dragons
and Hippogriffs entered communities poorly equipped to distinguish one magical
danger from another. A frightening encounter might enlarge suspicion of magic
and of the people believed to practise it. The dates are broad, but the result
is familiar enough: incomplete knowledge could make the extraordinary more
alarming, not less.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-003, fb-ch02-004 -->

Persecution alone does not explain why four practitioners became educators, or
why their answer took the form of a school. It does explain why that school
could not safely be merely a large house in a busy settlement. Distance reduced
casual observation, gave room for repeated practice and allowed magical
children to gather without every lesson becoming a public demonstration. The
castle's situation joined educational ambition to the requirements of
survival.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Secrecy did not mean isolation. Hogwarts had to remain connected to the society
it served. Pupils were sought and brought to it; people, messages and supplies
crossed the distance between the castle and the communities beyond. Remoteness
controlled access rather than ending it. A hidden school could reach out
because its centre was protected.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Safety was not the school's sole importance. A refuge may preserve people for
a season; a school must preserve knowledge through repetition. The remote site
supplied time as well as distance: time for instruction to recur, for errors to
be corrected and for one group of pupils to be followed by another. The castle
made continuity material.

### A Place for Learning

At the threshold of Hogwarts, scattered features become an institution. There
is a fixed place, more than one accomplished teacher, an intention to seek
young people showing magic, and a continuing purpose of education. The
founders did not invent magical childhood, skilled practice, runes, wands or
the need for discretion. Their deliberate combination of place, teachers and
pupils was new in consequence, whether or not it was unique in form.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, ext-a17-002, ps-ch05-007, ext-a41-001 -->

The castle gave knowledge an address. A craft carried in memory may disappear
when its keeper dies or withholds it; a practice attached to one household may
remain local. A school can assemble several practices, repeat instruction and
retain habits beyond the lives of particular teachers. It can also bring into
conversation methods that developed apart. Hogwarts did not instantly produce
the mature curriculum of later centuries, but it created the setting in which
magical education could acquire a history of its own.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, ext-a17-002, qtta-ch01-002, ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002, ext-a42-002 -->

Recruitment mattered just as much. By seeking children who displayed magic,
the founders reached beyond their immediate company. The record does not define
the first catchment or promise that every magical child was found. A child's
access to teaching no longer depended entirely upon appearing at the right
door; the new institution accepted some responsibility for finding the pupil.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Bringing pupils together changed the possibilities of learning. A child could
observe not only an experienced adult but other learners: their errors,
aptitudes and different inheritances of knowledge. Teachers could repeat a
lesson, discover where explanation failed and distinguish an individual
difficulty from a common one. These are ordinary consequences of collective
education, not a recovered timetable from the first year.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

The institution also made disagreement durable. Teachers who might once have
parted had bound their educational purposes to one place and to successive
groups of children. Their differences now had to be negotiated, expressed in
practice or allowed to threaten the common enterprise. The offices, customs
and divisions of later Hogwarts need not be imagined complete at the beginning
for that change to matter.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Permanence is a judgement supplied by what followed. A castle alone does not
guarantee an institution's future, but a durable site, active recruitment and
repeated instruction made survival beyond the founders possible. Once pupils
became former pupils, the school could extend through people as well as stone:
its knowledge, loyalties and disputes returned with them to the wider magical
community.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Nothing in these conditions made Hogwarts inevitable. Broom flight did not
require a school; Linfred's remedies flourished outside one; Muggle fear did
not require four particular founders; magical children did not require a
castle. The institution was one answer to a world of dispersed knowledge,
uneven access and dangerous visibility. It became historically decisive
because that answer endured.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002, ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002, cos-ch09-003 -->

Only at this point do the four founders properly enter the history. Their later
fame makes their cooperation appear natural and their divisions foreseeable.
Neither impression should be trusted without examination. The world that made
their school significant can now be seen in outline; the next question is who
they were, what purposes they brought to the undertaking, and how much of their
familiar character belongs to evidence rather than to the long memory of
Hogwarts.
