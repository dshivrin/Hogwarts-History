# Chapter One

## Before Hogwarts

### The History That Does Not Survive

The history of a school begins, inconveniently, before there is a school to
keep its history. Once an institution has acquired a building, officers,
customs and pupils, it also acquires the means by which its past may be
preserved. Before that point there may be memories, possessions, family
traditions and scattered references in quite unrelated records, but there is
rarely an archive. Hogwarts is no exception. Its earliest institutional
history, though uncertain in important particulars, is more readily described
than the educational world from which it emerged.

This imbalance has encouraged a good many confident pictures of Britain before
Hogwarts. Children are variously imagined at the elbows of learned parents, in
the service of travelling masters, gathered in small local schools, or left to
discover the limits of their powers unaided. Any of these arrangements is
possible. Possibility, however, is a generous host and a poor witness. No
surviving evidence establishes any one of them as the ordinary practice of the
period, still less as a system shared throughout magical Britain.

The secure account begins close to the point at which this obscurity ends.
Hogwarts was founded more than a thousand years ago, although the precise date
was already uncertain to later historians. Four witches and wizards built a
castle away from Muggle observation and brought to it young people who had
shown signs of magic, there to be educated. The record thus tells us something
of the pupils, the place and the purpose. It says almost nothing of the lessons
those pupils had received before they were found.

Even the phrase *before Hogwarts* requires care. It does not name a single age
with settled conditions. It covers an unknown succession of households and
communities, changing relations with Muggles, and an undetermined variety of
magical practice. Nor did all earlier habits necessarily vanish when the first
pupils entered the castle. Institutions seldom abolish the life around them;
they select from it, organise parts of it and, in time, persuade later
generations that what has become familiar must always have existed.

The temptation is therefore to reason backwards from the school. Because
Hogwarts eventually taught particular branches of magic, it is easy to provide
the imagined pre-Hogwarts child with miniature versions of its subjects.
Because the founders later sought pupils beyond their immediate company, it is
easy to picture every earlier magical family as waiting for an institution not
yet conceived. Both habits give the past a neatness it has not earned. They
replace a historical question—how was magical knowledge actually
transmitted?—with a modern timetable written in antique ink.

The bias of the evidence deserves emphasis. Schools preserve the matters that
schools consider important: admission, teaching, authority, inheritance and
custom. The knowledge passed quietly between two members of a household may
leave no comparable trace, especially when it creates neither office nor
dispute. We should therefore expect the record to magnify the institution once
it appears. That expectation explains the silence more sensibly than any claim
that the founding of Hogwarts marked the invention of magical teaching itself.

What can be recovered is narrower. Magical ability was present in children
before they came under the founders' instruction: the founders are said to
have sought youngsters who already displayed it. Manifestation is not the same
as mastery, however, and recognition is not education. A child's uncontrolled
or untrained magic may reveal whom to teach without revealing how teaching had
previously been done. The distinction is essential, for otherwise the evidence
for finding pupils is made to bear the quite different burden of describing
their upbringing.

The first conclusion must consequently be a modest one. Britain before
Hogwarts possessed magical children and accomplished adult practitioners, but
the path between the two is hidden from us. That path certainly existed in
some form. Great skill does not descend complete upon a witch or wizard, and
the founders themselves belonged to a learned magical world. Yet the surviving
account permits no map of its stages. The absence is not evidence that
education was absent; it is evidence that we do not know its ordinary shape.

### Learning Before the School: Evidence and Analogy

Later magical childhood offers useful comparisons, provided comparison is not
mistaken for ancestry. For centuries, young witches and wizards have
encountered a portion of their culture through stories told in the home. The
tales associated with Beedle became familiar bedtime reading and were known to
many pupils before, or independently of, their formal studies. Such stories
could do more than entertain. They conveyed judgements about conduct and, in at
least one well-attested instance, taught children that magic could not undo
death.

<!-- evidence: beedle-fm-002, beedle-ch04-001 -->

This is education in a broad but meaningful sense. A child who learns the
limits of magic from a tale has acquired no spell, yet has been introduced to
a rule by which magical action is understood. The distinction between cultural
formation and technical instruction is particularly useful here. Families may
transmit expectations, cautions, stories and habits without maintaining a
curriculum; a shared body of childhood lore may prepare pupils for school
without being a school in miniature.

It would nevertheless be unsafe to place Beedle beside the first generation of
Hogwarts pupils. The long circulation of his tales belongs to later centuries
and demonstrates a durable means of informal learning, not a practice securely
dated before the foundation. It shows what a magical household *can* transmit.
It does not show what most households transmitted in the founders' age, nor
whether such instruction was common, consistent or sufficient.

The questions multiply as soon as the evidence is pressed further. Were
children taught only by their own kin? Could a family without an accomplished
witch or wizard call upon a neighbour? Were skills guarded as family property,
exchanged among communities, or taught for payment? Did children learn wand
use early, late, or not at all? Nothing in the surviving account supplies a
general answer. The historian may ask these questions because each describes a
possible route by which knowledge passes. The historian may not convert the
questions into customs.

There is a further difficulty in the word *education*. Later centuries have
made it nearly synonymous with attendance, courses and measured progress. An
earlier household might have distinguished no such sphere from work, healing,
defence or ordinary magical competence. If so, even a fortunate surviving
reference might not call its subject education. This possibility widens the
search but proves nothing by itself. It reminds us that the absence of a
school-shaped account may reflect both missing evidence and the wrong question
being put to the evidence.

Nor should later school practice be allowed to answer on behalf of the earlier
world. The subjects, examinations and progression now associated with a
Hogwarts education are products of an institution with a long history. Even
where a branch of magic is ancient, its arrangement as a school subject need
not be. Knowledge may precede the name of a discipline; a discipline may
precede the classroom in which it is eventually taught. To assume that the
founders inherited a tidy set of subjects is merely to let the mature school
write its own preface.

The same caution applies to wands. The antiquity of wandmaking establishes the
antiquity of a craft, not the existence of a standard course in wand use.
Wandmakers, owners and teachers perform different offices, however often later
schooling brings their concerns together. Without evidence of who placed wands
in children's hands, at what age, and under whose supervision, the place of
the wand in pre-Hogwarts education must remain open.

We are left, then, with a distinction rather than a reconstruction. Informal
learning is not imaginary: later wizarding culture preserves clear examples of
it. But the bridge from those examples to the age before Hogwarts is missing.
This does not make the period empty. It makes the foundation more historically
interesting, because the new school can be assessed only by what it is known to
have gathered together, not by a convenient catalogue of what it supposedly
replaced.

### A World Not Yet Apart

The social setting is somewhat better illuminated than the educational one.
Before the International Statute of Secrecy, wizards and Muggles mingled with a
freedom that later generations can easily underestimate. Magical people might
defend themselves with swords as well as wands, and conventional swordsmanship
was not regarded as beneath an accomplished wizard. Such evidence belongs to a
long pre-Statute period and cannot describe every district or generation, but
it is sufficient to disprove the simplest picture of two worlds already sealed
from one another.

<!-- evidence: ext-a08-003 -->

Muggle records preserve another side of the same proximity. Medieval art and
literature retained memories of magical creatures, although often in distorted
form. Bestiaries confused one creature with another or mistook the magical for
the mundane; artistic representations combined observation, report and
imagination with very little concern for later classification. Their errors do
not make them worthless. Misidentification is itself evidence that something
had been seen, heard of, or imperfectly remembered.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002 -->

These sources must be handled with double caution. A creature seen by a Muggle
does not establish friendship with a wizard, and a carved or painted likeness
cannot tell us how magical families conducted their daily affairs. Conversely,
an inaccurate likeness does not prove complete ignorance. The period appears
neither fully open nor fully concealed. Magical beings and practices entered
Muggle knowledge unevenly, and that knowledge could be simultaneously real,
partial and wrong.

Nor are Muggle errors merely quaint decorations around wizarding history.
Classification governs response. What is mistaken for a monstrous animal, an
ill omen or a fraud will be approached differently from what is recognised as
the work of a magical neighbour. The bestiaries cannot tell us which response
followed in a particular place, but they show how observation could pass into
misdescription before it passed into memory. That process helps explain why
contact did not necessarily produce understanding.

Such conditions make local variation almost certain, though its extent cannot
be measured. A witch living among Muggle neighbours, a wizard belonging to an
old magical household, and a child whose ability appeared where no magical kin
were known would not necessarily have experienced the same degree of exposure.
Yet it would be a further error to supply each of them with an invented life in
order to make the variation vivid. The broad conclusion is enough: the later
legal division of the worlds should not be projected unchanged into the age
that preceded it.

This mixed world matters to the history of education because teaching requires
more than knowledge. It requires a place in which knowledge may be passed on,
people who can meet, and some degree of safety while they do so. Where magical
life touched the non-magical world, instruction might be observed, interrupted
or misunderstood. This is a reasonable consequence of contact, not evidence
for any particular interruption. No record permits us to people the early
history with suspicious neighbours at every window.

There is also no reason to suppose that contact produced only hostility. The
use of shared weapons and ordinary social exchange indicates practical
accommodation as well as difference. Fear and familiarity frequently occupy
the same street. A community may trade with, work beside or imitate people whom
it does not fully understand. When relations deteriorate, the very closeness
that once permitted exchange may provide the occasions for suspicion.

The value of this evidence lies precisely in its refusal of a simple story.
Hogwarts did not arise in a world where every magical person was already
hidden, nor can it be treated merely as the first wall between fixed opposing
societies. The institution emerged from a more unsettled relation: magic was
visible enough to be remembered and misunderstood, and magical people were
close enough to Muggles for ordinary contact and danger alike to be possible.

### Fear and the Uses of Distance

The most direct account of the foundation describes an age in which common
people feared magic and witches and wizards suffered considerable persecution.
It is in this context that the castle's remoteness is explained. The founders
built far from prying Muggle eyes. The evidence is firmest about that choice of
distance; it is less exact about the chronology, extent and causes of the
persecution itself.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Other medieval evidence helps to explain how fear could grow. Imperfect
understanding of magical creatures did not always remain a harmless error in a
bestiary. Sightings of conspicuous beasts could intensify alarm about magic and
those who practised it. This account supplies a general mechanism by which
misunderstanding became hostility, though its dating is broad and it must not
be made into a record of the precise year in which the founders acted.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-004 -->

The older evidence of mingling and the evidence of persecution are not mutually
exclusive. Neither friendship nor hostility observes a universal calendar.
Relations can differ from place to place and alter within a generation; open
contact can itself create both familiarity and occasions for fear. The records
do not allow those changes to be traced closely, but they warn against the
notion that one harmonious age ended neatly and a persecuting age began on a
known date.

<!-- evidence: ext-a08-003, cos-ch09-003, fb-ch02-004 -->

It is tempting to make persecution the complete explanation of Hogwarts. The
result is a powerful tale: danger scatters the magical community until four
protectors gather its children behind enchanted walls. The surviving account
supports only part of this. It explicitly connects Muggle fear and persecution
with the decision to build the castle out of sight. It does not state that
persecution alone caused the founders to establish a school, that every pupil
was personally endangered, or that the founders acted under a single agreed
programme of rescue.

The narrower conclusion is also the more useful. Whatever other purposes moved
the founders, a remote site made sustained collective education less exposed
to Muggle observation. A lesson given to one child in a household might attract
little notice; a permanent gathering of many magical children and accomplished
teachers presented a different problem. The institution that concentrated
education also concentrated risk. Distance was therefore not an incidental
feature of the school. It was one of the conditions under which the school
could endure.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Endurance has educational consequences of its own. A protected place permits
lessons to be repeated, corrected and handed from one group of pupils to the
next. It also permits teachers to disagree without dissolving the whole act of
instruction at the end of a visit. These are institutional possibilities, not
details recorded of the first school years. They explain why the choice of a
durable and defensible place was more than architectural: it made continuity
possible even though the early form of that continuity is unknown.

This does not mean that remoteness amounted to complete withdrawal. Pupils had
to be found and brought to the castle. Knowledge, messages and people had to
cross the space between the school and the communities from which its pupils
came. The surviving account offers no procedure for this movement, but the
fact of recruitment prevents us from imagining Hogwarts as a sealed refuge
unconcerned with the world beyond it. Concealment protected an outward-looking
educational purpose.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Here the difference between secrecy and isolation becomes important. Secrecy
controls knowledge of a place or practice; isolation abandons connection.
Hogwarts required the former precisely because it could not accept the latter.
A school without pupils is only a building, however excellent its defences.
The founders' achievement at this threshold lay not simply in going somewhere
remote, but in making remoteness compatible with the repeated gathering of
young witches and wizards.

### At the Threshold of an Institution

At the edge of the foundation, the evidence becomes suddenly more concrete.
There is a castle built together by four outstanding magical people. There are
youngsters who show signs of magic. There is a deliberate act of seeking them
out, bringing them to one place and educating them there. These are elementary
facts, but in combination they describe an institutional change of great
consequence.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

The castle gave instruction a durable centre. Active recruitment connected
that centre to children beyond the founders' immediate households. The
presence of several founders brought accomplished practitioners into a common
enterprise. None of this proves that earlier teaching was wholly private, that
no other school existed, or that the first arrangements resembled the Hogwarts
of later centuries. It does establish something more substantial than the
occasional passing on of knowledge: a continuing place organised for the
education of successive pupils.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

“Permanent” must be understood here as a historical judgement, not as a boast
attributed to the founders. They could not know every danger the school would
survive, nor does the record tell us that permanence was declared at the
outset. The judgement arises from the conjunction of a castle, a school and a
practice of bringing children to it, confirmed by the institution's long
continuance. What began was capable of outlasting the first teachers and first
pupils, even if its eventual form was not present from the beginning.

With continuity came the possibility of an institutional memory distinct from
any one family's recollection. A repeated gathering can compare instruction,
preserve decisions and establish expectations that remain when individuals
depart. We do not know how quickly Hogwarts acquired such habits, and the first
generation should not be credited with the records of later centuries. Yet the
fixed school created the conditions in which magical education could begin to
possess a history of its own.

The importance of recruitment deserves equal care. Seeking out young people
who showed magic enlarged the founders' educational field beyond whoever
happened already to stand beside them. Yet the evidence does not say how far
they travelled, how children were identified, which families consented, or
whether any were refused. It would be premature to claim a universal mission.
The act matters because it made education an undertaking that reached for
pupils, rather than merely waiting for instruction to be requested.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

This change also created new historical problems. A fixed school must decide
what is taught, who may enter, how authority is divided and how differences
among teachers are to be contained. Those questions belong to the history of
Hogwarts, but their later answers must not be smuggled into its first purpose.
At this stage the evidence gives us no timetable, no developed offices and no
complete rule of admission. It gives us a shared undertaking whose internal
shape remains to be examined.

There is no inevitability in this threshold. Muggle fear did not dictate that
four particular people would cooperate; the existence of magical children did
not require a castle; the remoteness of a site did not guarantee that pupils
could be brought safely to it. Other answers to these circumstances were
possible, and some may have been attempted without leaving a trace. Hogwarts
became historically decisive because one answer endured, not because it was
the only answer that could have been imagined.

The world before Hogwarts therefore remains partly hidden, but it is not
without outline. Magical knowledge existed without the school that would later
organise so much of it. Magical and non-magical people lived within sight of
one another, sometimes in ordinary contact and sometimes in fear. Children
showed powers before any institutional hand reached them. Against this uneven
background, a remote place was built and an educational purpose attached to
it.

<!-- evidence: ext-a08-003, fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002, fb-ch02-004, cos-ch09-003 -->

Only then do the founders properly enter the history. Their later fame can make
their meeting appear preordained and their differences appear visible from the
first. Neither assumption is safe. Before considering what they built together,
we must consider who they were, what may genuinely be known of them, and how
much of their familiar image belongs not to record but to the long memory of
the school they founded.
