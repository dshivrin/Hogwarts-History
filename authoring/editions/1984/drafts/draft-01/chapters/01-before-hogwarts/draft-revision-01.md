# Chapter One

## Before Hogwarts

### Before the School

The history of Hogwarts begins before Hogwarts had a history of its own. A
school preserves registers, rules, appointments, disputes and recollections;
the world preceding it leaves no such convenient deposit. What survives from
that world is scattered among later histories, old objects, specialist books
and the imperfect notice of Muggles. The fragments are unequal, but they are
not silent. They describe a magical society already in possession of skilled
arts, already sharing Britain with its non-magical neighbours, and already
facing the difficulty of remaining visible without becoming vulnerable.

Hogwarts is older than the present millennium. Its precise year of foundation
was uncertain even in the account most commonly taught at the school, and no
day or season can responsibly be supplied. The secure outline is spare. Four
witches and wizards built a castle far from Muggle observation, sought out
young people who showed signs of magic, brought them to that place and educated
them there. The founders, the gathering of pupils, the remoteness of the site
and the purpose of instruction belong to history; much of the picturesque
detail later attached to them belongs elsewhere.

That outline is enough to correct one persistent illusion. Hogwarts did not
bring magic into an empty country. The people who made the school were already
accomplished practitioners, and the children they sought displayed magic
before the school received them. Knowledge, skill and magical identity
therefore existed on both sides of the foundation. What changed was not the
existence of magic but the manner in which part of magical society attempted to
preserve and transmit it.

The distinction matters because schools tend to annex the past in retrospect.
Once generations have learnt Potions, Charms or Herbology under those names,
the subjects acquire the appearance of timeless divisions. Earlier practice
need not have been so neatly arranged. The ability to recognise a useful plant
may be older than a discipline devoted to plants; a healing preparation may be
older than the classroom in which its ingredients are later memorised. The
school subject is an arrangement of knowledge, not proof of the knowledge's
beginning.

The surviving material does not reveal a general system of education before
Hogwarts. No earlier British school, common curriculum or ordinary course of
childhood instruction can presently be described. That limitation should be
stated once and then allowed to do its work. It forbids an invented procession
of village masters and family academies; it does not require us to imagine that
each generation rediscovered magic afresh. A society capable of producing the
founders had plainly learnt how to carry difficult knowledge across time, even
if the routes by which it travelled have mostly disappeared.

### Old Arts

One of the clearest survivals is the broom. A European manuscript records
witches and wizards using flying broomsticks in 962. The early broom was no
polished racing instrument: the surviving description gives it one forward
speed and the ability to rise, descend and stop, with little comfort and less
grace. Its importance lies precisely in this roughness. In the same broad age
from which Hogwarts emerged, magical people were adapting an ordinary object
to flight, repeating the craft well enough for its use to be recorded, and
valuing it as something portable and easily hidden. The uncertain foundation
date prevents us from deciding whether the manuscript just precedes or just
follows the school's opening.

The date provides a rare fixed point in an otherwise imprecise early record. It
also reveals a relation between magical invention and social pressure. A broom
did not merely carry its rider; it could be put away. The choice of a discreet
means of flight indicates that Muggle observation was a practical concern
before the comprehensive law of secrecy and long before modern concealment
became an administrative system. Fear of discovery was not yet the same thing
as legal separation, but it could already influence the objects magical people
made and used.

Broom flight also represents accumulated knowledge rather than an isolated
spell. Timber had to be selected, worked and charmed; a device had to respond
predictably enough for a rider to entrust body and destination to it. The
limited controls of the tenth-century broom show a craft in development. They
suggest makers improving upon earlier work and users learning the habits of an
imperfect instrument. No school is required for such improvement, but neither
is solitary inspiration a sufficient explanation for a technology already
present in Europe.

The broom is valuable evidence because it is so thoroughly practical. It was
not preserved to commemorate a ruler, battle or celebrated enchantment. Its
rough handle and restricted movement record the less splendid work by which a
magical society made distance manageable. A tool of this kind joins invention
to repetition: it must be made more than once, controlled more than once and
judged against earlier examples. Whatever its exact relation to the school's
opening, the object was carrying knowledge from one pair of hands to another.

Wands offer a second, less precisely documented example. A later account of
the founders' period remembers accomplished wizards handling conventional
weapons as well as wands: swordsmanship and wand use belonged to overlapping
forms of defence rather than to separate worlds. The Ollivander establishment,
for its part, publicly dates its wandmaking name to 382 BC. That date is a
maker's claim, not an unbroken ledger of every craftsman in the intervening
centuries, but it places specialist wandcraft within a historical tradition
far older than Hogwarts.

Here craft and magical practice meet. Wandmaking depends upon timber and
magical cores, judgement of materials and knowledge of the relation between an
instrument and its owner. The founder-period evidence does not tell us when a
child first held a wand or who taught its use. It does show that the founders
did not invent the tool through which so much later school magic would be
performed. Hogwarts inherited a material culture as surely as it inherited
spells.

Other familiar arts are harder to carry back across the foundation. Later
records make the practical alliance of plants, potions and healing abundantly
clear, but the evidence presently assembled for this early period supplies no
secure date for their beginnings. Their antiquity may be plausible; it is not
therefore dated. This is an important difference between a history of magical
knowledge and a backwards copy of the modern timetable. Brooms and wands enter
the present account because early evidence bears their weight. Potions and
plant medicine must await evidence equal to the confidence with which they are
usually imagined.

Creature knowledge occupies a different position. Dragons, griffins,
unicorns, phoenixes and centaurs appear in medieval Muggle art and literature,
often in distorted forms. Their appearance does not establish a medieval
curriculum of creature study, but it does establish encounters, reports and
memories persistent enough to cross from magical nature into non-magical
culture. A creature need not have been correctly classified to become part of
the knowledge of an age.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002 -->

Taken together, these fragments show a culture of use rather than a list of
school subjects. People flew, worked wood and magical materials, used wands,
carried ordinary weapons and encountered dangerous beings whose traces their
neighbours recorded under uncertain names. Hogwarts would later bring many
kinds of knowledge under one institutional roof. It did not create the world
on which that roof was raised.

### Knowledge in Use

The practical arts imply teachers in the broadest sense, although they do not
identify a profession of teaching. A broommaker could not maintain a craft
without communicating choices of wood, charm and balance. A rider could not
make much use of a broom without learning its controls. Wandcraft required
materials to be found and judged before they could be fashioned. These forms
of knowledge may have passed through families, workshops, neighbours or
temporary partnerships. The evidence does not choose a single route, and
ordinary life probably supplied more than one.

This was learning without any necessary distinction between education and
work. The acquisition of a skill while assisting an experienced practitioner
would leave a different record from attendance at a school, if it left one at
all. So would instruction given in response to an accident, a journey or a
seasonal task. The point is not to populate Britain with undocumented
apprenticeships. It is to recognise that practice itself creates occasions on
which knowledge must be shown, corrected and repeated.

Objects could preserve part of that instruction. An old broom embodied choices
about balance and control whether or not its maker wrote them down; an
unsuccessful one supplied a lesson of a more bruising kind. A wand joined
particular materials in a form that another maker could examine, imitate or
reject. Material craft is not a substitute for speech or demonstration, but it
gives knowledge a life beyond memory alone. This helps explain how a dispersed
culture could accumulate skill without leaving anything resembling a national
syllabus.

Children also entered this world before anyone called them pupils. The
founders sought young people who already showed magic; recognition therefore
preceded institutional instruction. Manifestation was not mastery, but it gave
families and communities something to interpret. Where magical knowledge was
available nearby, an extraordinary event could be recognised as part of a
known life rather than treated as chaos. How much technical guidance followed
must have depended partly upon who was present to provide it.

Later wizarding childhood gives a modest illustration of another channel.
Stories associated with Beedle became bedtime reading across centuries, and a
well-known tale taught many children that magic could not reverse death. No
spell was learnt from that lesson, yet a limit upon magical action was. The
example comes from a later age and does not place Beedle in the homes of the
founders' first pupils. It demonstrates something narrower and more useful:
magical culture can be transmitted domestically through memory and story as
well as through formal instruction.

<!-- evidence: beedle-fm-002, beedle-ch04-001 -->

Technical skill and cultural judgement are not the same kind of knowledge,
but a school eventually requires both. It must teach what may be done, what is
dangerous, what is forbidden and what is impossible. Before such teaching was
collected, those judgements belonged to the scattered settings in which magic
was practised. A warning attached to a tool, a story attached to a fear, or a
maker's correction attached to a failed object could each preserve more than a
bare fact. They could preserve conduct.

The unevenness of such transmission is likely to have been as important as its
forms. Knowledge held by a particular family or craft could travel only as far
as its keepers allowed or circumstances carried it. A gifted child born close
to experienced practitioners began from a different position from one whose
magic appeared where little magical knowledge was at hand. The record gives no
measure of that inequality, but the founders' decision to seek children rather
than wait for them is intelligible against it. Recruitment extended instruction
beyond the accidents of immediate acquaintance.

Hogwarts thus arose from a world in which magical knowledge was real,
consequential and dispersed. Some of it belonged to tools; some to skilled
hands; some to stories and cautions. The school's eventual achievement was to
make transmission repeatable across generations of pupils. Before considering
that change, however, the physical world in which dispersed learning occurred
must be restored, for magical Britain did not occupy a separate island.

### Among Muggles

Magical and non-magical Britain shared roads, fields, forests and settlements.
They did not share an equal understanding of what those places contained. The
broom record of 962 shows magical travellers already taking concealment into
account, while medieval Muggle sources show that concealment was imperfect.
Creatures passed into art, manuscript and tale even when their names and
natures did not.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002, fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002 -->

One fragment makes the process unusually visible. A Muggle writer remembered
as Brother Benedict described a violent encounter in a herb garden with what
he took to be a monstrous ferret. Later magical interpretation identifies the
animal as a Jarvey, probably pursuing gnomes. The incident is small, but small
incidents are often what broad histories lack. A cultivated garden, a magical
creature and a bewildered observer occupied the same patch of ground; the
record preserved the encounter while misunderstanding almost every part of it.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-003 -->

Such records do not prove that the two populations lived in easy intimacy.
They prove proximity. A Muggle might know that something extraordinary had
been seen without knowing which creature it was, whether a witch or wizard was
nearby, or how the event fitted into a larger magical world. Magical people,
meanwhile, could move through the same landscape while calculating what might
be noticed. Knowledge was asymmetrical: one community possessed names and
techniques that the other often replaced with marvel, monster or mistake.

Accounts from the much longer period before the International Statute of
Secrecy describe witches and wizards mingling freely with Muggles and using
swords as well as wands for defence. This evidence establishes historical
permeability, not a uniform portrait of the founders' own decade. The centuries
before statutory separation contained many local arrangements and many degrees
of contact. The dated broom record and medieval bestiaries allow a firmer
statement: around the broad period in which Hogwarts emerged, magical people
were neither wholly public nor wholly removed from Muggle sight.

<!-- evidence: ext-a08-003, qtta-ch01-002, fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002 -->

That middle condition shaped everyday choices. A portable broom answered the
problem of magical travel without leaving a conspicuous beast or vehicle to be
explained. A remote place could protect repeated magical activity more
effectively than a site surrounded by casual witnesses. Neither choice amounts
to the later Statute in miniature. They belong to an older politics of
discretion, practised through objects and places before secrecy acquired its
international legal machinery.

Muggle testimony must consequently be read for contact as much as for error.
The wrong name in a bestiary may conceal a correct observation of size,
movement or danger. A fantastic image may preserve the report of a creature
long after direct experience has been converted into folklore. These sources
are unreliable guides to magical classification but valuable evidence of what
could not be kept entirely out of view.

The relationship was not static. Familiarity could make an unusual neighbour
less alarming, while a frightening encounter could attach suspicion to magic
more generally. Different responses could coexist, and a record of persecution
need not cancel a record of contact. Indeed, persecution requires some form of
recognition, however mistaken. People do not fear an invisible population in
the same way that they fear powers glimpsed but not understood.

This is the historical setting in which magical learning acquired a problem
of place. A single act of instruction might pass unnoticed. The repeated
gathering of magical children, teachers, equipment and practice was harder to
hide. The more successful a shared centre became, the more visible its traffic
and effects might be. An institution promised continuity, but continuity also
required protection.

### Fear and Distance

The strongest founding account places Muggle fear and the persecution of
witches and wizards beside the choice of a remote site. The founders built away
from prying eyes. The statement does not give a map of persecutions or assign
every attack to a year, but its institutional meaning is plain: concealment was
part of the castle's historical purpose, not a picturesque quality acquired by
later legend.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Medieval creature history helps explain how fear could spread. Reports of
dragons and Hippogriffs entered societies poorly equipped to distinguish one
magical danger from another. Misunderstanding did not remain safely between the
covers of a bestiary; sightings could enlarge hostility towards magic and
towards those believed to practise it. The chronology is broad, but the social
mechanism is credible and well attested: incomplete knowledge made the
extraordinary more alarming, not less.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-003, fb-ch02-004 -->

Coexistence and persecution were therefore not two tidy ages separated by a
single change of mind. Contact provided occasions for recognition,
misinterpretation, curiosity and fear, and responses could differ by place as
well as change over time. No invented catalogue of raids is needed to
understand the pressure. Magical practice was visible enough to attract
danger, and a permanent concentration of it demanded more than the discretion
of an individual household.

Persecution alone does not explain why four practitioners chose to become
educators, or why their answer took the form of a school. It does explain why
their school could not safely be merely a large house in a busy settlement.
Distance reduced casual observation, provided room for repeated practice and
allowed magical children to gather without every lesson becoming a public
demonstration. The castle's situation joined educational ambition to the
practical requirements of survival.

The distinction between secrecy and isolation is important here. Hogwarts had
to remain connected to the society it served. Pupils were sought and brought
to it; messages, supplies and people necessarily crossed the distance between
the castle and the communities beyond. Remoteness controlled access rather
than ending it. A hidden school could reach outward because its centre was
protected.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

The early broom offers a useful parallel. Its value lay partly in being
ordinary enough to conceal and portable enough to remove, yet it enlarged the
distance a magical traveller could cross. The same conjunction appears on a
larger scale at Hogwarts. Concealment did not merely shrink magical life. Used
intelligently, it created a secure point from which magical life could be
extended and renewed.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002, cos-ch09-003 -->

Safety was not the school's sole historical importance. A refuge may preserve
people for a season; a school must preserve knowledge through repetition. The
remote site supplied time as well as distance: time for instruction to recur,
for errors to be corrected, for several teachers to compare practice, and for
one group of pupils to be followed by another. The castle made continuity
material.

### A Place for Learning

At the threshold of Hogwarts, scattered features become an institution. There
is a fixed place, more than one accomplished teacher, an intention to seek
young people showing magic, and a continuing purpose of education. The
founders did not invent magical childhood, skilled practice, the wand or the
need for discretion. Their deliberate combination of place, teachers and
pupils was new in consequence, whether or not it was unique in form.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, ext-a08-003, ps-ch05-007 -->

The castle gave knowledge an address. A craft carried in memory may disappear
when its keeper dies or withholds it; a practice attached to one household may
remain local. A school can assemble several practices, repeat instruction and
retain habits beyond the lives of particular teachers. It can also compare
methods that had previously developed apart. Hogwarts did not instantly
produce the mature curriculum of later centuries, but it created the setting
in which magical education could accumulate an institutional history.

Recruitment was equally consequential. By seeking children who displayed
magic, the founders made instruction an undertaking that reached beyond their
immediate company. The record does not define the first catchment or promise
that every magical child was found. Its significance is more practical. A
child's access to teaching no longer depended entirely upon appearing at the
right door; the institution accepted some responsibility for finding the
pupil.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Bringing pupils together changed the nature of learning. A child could observe
not only an experienced adult but other learners: their errors, aptitudes and
different inheritances of knowledge. Teachers could repeat a lesson, discover
where explanation failed and distinguish an individual difficulty from a
general one. These are ordinary consequences of collective education, not a
recovered timetable from the first year. They help explain why a permanent
school could become more than a sheltered collection of private lessons.

The institution also made disagreement durable. In an informal arrangement,
teachers who differed could simply part; in a shared school, their differences
had to be negotiated, expressed in practice or allowed to threaten the common
enterprise. Hogwarts would eventually acquire offices, customs and divisions
through which authority was exercised. None need be imagined complete at the
beginning. The decisive change was that several powerful practitioners had
bound their educational purposes to one place and to successive groups of
children.

Permanence is a judgement supplied by what followed. The founders could not
have known every danger their school would survive, and a castle alone does
not guarantee an institution's future. Yet a durable site, active recruitment
and repeated instruction made survival beyond the founders possible. Once
pupils became former pupils, the school could extend through people as well as
stone: its knowledge, loyalties and disputes returned with them to the wider
magical community.

Nothing in these conditions made Hogwarts inevitable. Broom flight did not
require a school; Muggle fear did not require four particular founders;
magical children did not require a castle. The institution was one answer to a
world of dispersed knowledge, uneven access and dangerous visibility. It
became historically decisive because that answer endured.

Before Hogwarts, then, Britain was not waiting in ignorance. Witches and
wizards already possessed tools, crafts and formidable skill. Knowledge moved
through use and association, even though its ordinary paths are poorly
recorded. Magical creatures and magical travellers crossed a landscape shared
with Muggles, leaving traces in manuscripts, art and fear. What the school
added was a protected centre: a place to which children could be brought and
in which knowledge could be made to outlast the circumstances of its keeper.

Only at this point do the four founders properly enter the history. Their
later fame makes their cooperation appear natural and their divisions
foreseeable. Neither impression should be trusted without examination. The
world that made their school significant can now be seen in outline; the next
question is who they were, what purposes they brought to the undertaking, and
how much of their familiar character belongs to evidence rather than to the
long memory of Hogwarts.
