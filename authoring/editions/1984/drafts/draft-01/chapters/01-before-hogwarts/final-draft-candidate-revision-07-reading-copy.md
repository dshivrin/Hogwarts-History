# Chapter One

## Before Hogwarts

### Before the School

The history of Hogwarts begins before Hogwarts had a history of its own. A school preserves registers, rules, appointments, disputes and recollections; the world preceding it leaves no such convenient deposit. What survives from that world is scattered among later histories, old objects, specialist books and the imperfect notice of Muggles. The fragments are unequal, but they are not silent. They describe a magical society already in possession of skilled arts, already sharing Britain with its non-magical neighbours, and already facing the difficulty of remaining visible without becoming vulnerable.

Hogwarts is older than the present millennium. Its precise year of foundation was uncertain even in the account most commonly taught at the school, and no day or season can responsibly be supplied. The secure outline is spare. Four witches and wizards built a castle far from Muggle observation, sought out young people who showed signs of magic, brought them to that place and educated them there. The founders, the gathering of pupils, the remoteness of the site and the purpose of instruction belong to history; much of the picturesque detail later attached to them belongs elsewhere.

That outline corrects one persistent illusion. Hogwarts did not bring magic into an empty country. The people who made the school were already accomplished practitioners, and the children they sought displayed magic before the school received them. Knowledge, skill and magical identity therefore existed on both sides of the foundation. What changed was not the existence of magic but the way part of magical society attempted to preserve and transmit it.

Schools tend to annex the past in retrospect. Once generations have learnt Potions, Charms or Flying under those names, the subjects take on the appearance of timeless divisions. They were not. A broom did not wait for a Flying lesson before it carried a rider. The school subject is an arrangement of knowledge, not proof of the knowledge's beginning.

No general system of education before Hogwarts can now be described. No account of an earlier British school, a common curriculum or an ordinary course of childhood instruction survives. That forbids an invented procession of village masters and family academies; it does not require us to imagine that each generation rediscovered magic afresh. A society capable of producing the founders had plainly learnt how to carry difficult knowledge across time, even if most of the routes have disappeared.

### Old Arts

Among the oldest things at Hogwarts is an object that was old before the school existed. The Hogwarts Pensieve is made of ornately carved stone and engraved with modified Saxon runes. It is older than the castle that now contains it, though the engraving cannot be independently dated and the account does not say that the runes performed any magic. One tradition says that the founders found the object half-buried on the very spot where they chose to build. It is a fine story, but no record confirms it. The Pensieve's age does not prove that the founders found it there; what survives is still remarkable enough: an ancient magical object of carved stone endured into the institution that followed.

Another old craft comes to us by way of a less dignified object. A European manuscript records witches and wizards using flying broomsticks in 962. The early broom offered one forward speed and the ability to rise, descend and stop. It was uncomfortable, limited and unlikely to inspire nostalgia in anyone required to travel far upon it. It nevertheless shows an everyday magical technology in use and still being improved in the broad age from which Hogwarts emerged. The uncertain date of the school's foundation prevents us from saying whether the record falls just before or just after its opening.

The broom also joined travel to discretion. Unlike a conspicuous flying beast, it could be hidden without much difficulty, an advantage recognised long before secrecy became an international body of law. Timber had to be chosen, worked and charmed; controls had to answer reliably enough for a rider to trust them. The manuscript does not name the makers or their teachers, but a repeatable device already in use is evidence of knowledge passing between hands. Solitary inspiration may produce a marvel. A working craft requires memory.

Wandcraft carries a longer tradition, though not so tidy a chronology. The Ollivander shop sign claims a family wandmaking history from 382 BC, while Garrick Ollivander believed that his ancestors reached Britain with the Romans and sold crafted wands to British wizards whose own instruments were crude and unreliable. The shop's date is a public tradition, and the Roman story a descendant's belief; neither is an unbroken ledger from antiquity. The medieval record is firmer: Geraint Ollivander was a celebrated wandmaker whose written observations survived in later family practice. Only a minority of trees yield timber fit for a wand; recognising them takes long experience, and Bowtruckles may direct a trained eye towards a promising tree. Plant lore, creature lore and the maker's judgement thus met at the workbench before the wand performed any magic at all.

Creature knowledge survives differently. Dragons, griffins, unicorns, phoenixes and centaurs entered medieval Muggle art and writing, often in forms that would have dismayed a competent magizoologist. Their appearance records encounters, reports and memories strong enough to cross from magical nature into non-magical culture. A creature need not be correctly classified to become part of the knowledge of an age.

The broom and creature records do not add up to an early timetable. They show a culture of use: people flew, improved tools and learnt the habits of dangerous creatures. Hogwarts would eventually arrange many kinds of knowledge under one roof. It did not create the world on which that roof was raised.

### Knowledge in Use

Craft implies teaching in the broadest sense, though not necessarily a profession of teachers. A rider must learn how to rise and, more urgently, how to stop; a maker must learn how to produce controls reliable enough to trust. Tools, demonstrations and failed attempts could preserve parts of a craft without producing a school register.

Learning of this kind did not separate itself neatly from work. Selecting timber, shaping a broom or assisting an experienced hand could all provide instruction without becoming a formal lesson. An unsuccessful broom might teach balance in a particularly memorable fashion. Such means were local and uneven, but skill could accumulate through them.

Potioneering belonged to this practical world, but its earliest British history is obscure. No direct evidence establishes ordinary household potion-making in tenth-century Britain. Two centuries later, however, Linfred of Stinchcombe cultivated unusual plants, experimented behind closed doors and supplied medicinal help to Muggle neighbours, who took him for an eccentric but useful gardener. Wizarding historians credit him with remedies that developed into potions still known in later centuries, including the ancestors of Skele-gro and Pepperup Potion; he also sold his cures to other witches and wizards. The account does not reveal whether he wrote down his methods, demonstrated them or guarded them closely. Linfred is not a witness to the founders' century: his twelfth-century household instead shows that the castle had not swallowed every useful plant, healing practice or new preparation within its walls. His garden also belongs beside the wandmaker's woodpile. Gardens, woods and workshops did not observe the borders later drawn between Herbology, Potions, the study of creatures and wandcraft; useful knowledge met where the work required it.

Stories carried another kind of knowledge. In later centuries, tales associated with Beedle became familiar bedtime reading, and one well-known story taught many wizarding children that magic could not reverse death. No spell was learnt from that lesson, yet a limit upon magical action was. Beedle cannot be placed in the homes of the founders' first pupils, but his later reception shows how a magical culture may pass on judgement and restraint as well as technique.

The weakness of scattered teaching is easy to see. Knowledge held by one family, craft or gifted healer could travel only as far as its keepers and circumstances allowed. A child born near accomplished practitioners stood in a different position from one whose magic appeared where little help was at hand. The record gives no measure of that inequality, but the founders' decision to seek children rather than wait for them is intelligible against it.

Hogwarts thus arose from a world in which magical knowledge was real, consequential and dispersed. Some of it belonged to tools and the skilled hands that made them; some to observations of the magical world. The school created a place in which knowledge could be taught repeatedly. The people who possessed it, however, belonged to the same British landscape as their Muggle neighbours.

### Among Muggles

Magical and non-magical Britain shared roads, fields, forests, harbours and settlements. Before the International Statute of Secrecy, they also shared ordinary ways of getting about. Witches, wizards and Muggles travelled in horse-drawn carts and sailing ships; in defence, a wizard might use a sword as well as a wand. These accounts cover a broad pre-Statute past rather than the founders' particular decade, but they place magical people inside historical Britain, not beside it in a sealed and parallel country. Shared transport meant shared practical conditions: a cart followed a road, and a sailing ship depended upon harbours, crews and weather, however much magic might ease one part of the passage. The cauldron, too, once belonged to both populations. Muggles and magical people used the same suspended cooking pots before changing household technologies left the cauldron chiefly in wizarding hands, still well suited to potion-making over a naked flame. The source gives no century for this change and cannot furnish the founders' kitchen. It preserves a smaller history: ordinary objects could be shared, adapted and eventually come to look peculiarly magical.

The broom belongs to the same mixed landscape. Its rider used magic, but the object itself was commonplace enough to hide. Discretion worked through ordinary things: a broom that could be put away, a road travelled without display, a remote place chosen because repeated magic would be difficult to explain. Muggle observation was already a practical concern, though the two populations had not yet been divided by the later law of secrecy.

Muggle records show how imperfect that discretion could be. Medieval art and literature remembered magical creatures while confusing their names and natures. One writer known as Brother Benedict described a violent encounter in a herb garden with what he took to be a monstrous ferret. Later magical interpretation identifies a Jarvey, probably hunting gnomes. A cultivated garden, a magical creature and a bewildered observer occupied the same patch of ground; the record kept the incident and misunderstood nearly all of it.

Such records show proximity, not easy intimacy. A Muggle might glimpse something extraordinary without knowing its name or how it fitted into the larger magical world. Magical people possessed names and techniques that their neighbours replaced with marvel, monster or mistake. A shared landscape did not imply a shared understanding.

This was the setting in which magical learning acquired a problem of place. A single act of instruction or experiment might pass unnoticed. The repeated gathering of magical children, teachers, equipment and practice was harder to hide. Continuity needed somewhere to live, but a place made continuity visible.

### Fear and Distance

The strongest founding account places Muggle fear and the persecution of witches and wizards beside the choice of a remote site. The founders built away from prying eyes. What the account makes plain is that concealment belonged to the castle's purpose; it was not a picturesque quality supplied by later legend.

To a Muggle observer, a creature without a familiar name could become a monster in the telling. The same world that remembered a Jarvey as a monstrous ferret also carried reports of dragons and Hippogriffs. No particular persecution can be traced to such an encounter, but it is not difficult to see how fear could grow where marvel, danger and ignorance met.

Persecution alone does not explain why four practitioners became educators, or why their answer took the form of a school. It does explain why that school could not safely be merely a large house in a busy settlement. Distance reduced casual observation, gave room for repeated practice and allowed magical children to gather without every lesson becoming a public demonstration. The castle's situation joined educational ambition to the requirements of survival.

Secrecy did not mean isolation. Hogwarts had to remain connected to the society it served. Pupils were sought and brought to it, so remoteness controlled access rather than ending it. The evidence supports a protected centre that could gather learners; it does not recover the ordinary traffic by which that centre was maintained.

Safety was not the school's sole importance. A refuge may preserve people for a season; a school must preserve knowledge through repetition. The remote site supplied time as well as distance: time for instruction to recur, for errors to be corrected and for one group of pupils to be followed by another. The castle made continuity material.

### A Place for Learning

At the threshold of Hogwarts, scattered features become an institution. There is a fixed place, more than one accomplished teacher, an intention to seek young people showing magic, and a continuing purpose of education. Their deliberate combination of place, teachers and pupils was new in consequence, whether or not it was unique in form.

The castle gave knowledge an address. A craft carried in memory may disappear when its keeper dies or withholds it; a practice attached to one household may remain local. By assembling teachers and pupils in one enduring place, Hogwarts gave knowledge a chance to outlive individual families, teachers and practitioners. It did not instantly produce the mature curriculum of later centuries, but it created the setting in which magical education could acquire a history of its own.

Recruitment mattered just as much. By seeking children who displayed magic, the founders reached beyond their immediate company, though the record neither defines the first catchment nor promises that every magical child was found. The records do not show the first classroom at work. What they do show is a deliberate effort to gather children and educate them in one place. The daily arrangements by which that effort became a functioning school belong to the history of the institution itself.

Nothing in these conditions made Hogwarts inevitable. Broom flight did not require a school; Muggle fear did not require four particular founders; magical children did not require a castle. The institution was one answer to a world of dispersed knowledge, uneven access and dangerous visibility. It became historically decisive because that answer endured.

Only at this point do the four founders properly enter the history. Their later fame makes their cooperation appear natural and their divisions foreseeable. Neither impression should be trusted without examination. The world that made their school significant can now be seen in outline; the next question is who they were, what purposes they brought to the undertaking, and how much of their familiar character belongs to evidence rather than to the long memory of Hogwarts.
