# Chapter One

## Before Hogwarts

### Before the School

The history of Hogwarts begins before Hogwarts had a history of its own. A
school preserves registers, rules, appointments, disputes and recollections;
the world preceding it leaves no such convenient deposit. What survives from
that world is scattered among later histories, old objects, specialist books
and the imperfect notice of Muggles. The fragments are unequal, but they are
not silent. They describe a magical society already in possession of skilled
arts, already sharing Britain with its non-magical neighbours, and already
facing the difficulty of remaining visible without becoming vulnerable.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002, qtta-ch01-002, ext-a41-001, fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002, ext-a45-001, cos-ch09-003 -->

Hogwarts is older than the present millennium. Its precise year of foundation
was uncertain even in the account most commonly taught at the school, and no
day or season can responsibly be supplied. The secure outline is spare. Four
witches and wizards built a castle far from Muggle observation, sought out
young people who showed signs of magic, brought them to that place and educated
them there. The founders, the gathering of pupils, the remoteness of the site
and the purpose of instruction belong to history; much of the picturesque
detail later attached to them belongs elsewhere.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

That outline is enough to correct one persistent illusion. Hogwarts did not
bring magic into an empty country. The people who made the school were already
accomplished practitioners, and the children they sought displayed magic
before the school received them. Knowledge, skill and magical identity
therefore existed on both sides of the foundation. What changed was not the
existence of magic but the manner in which part of magical society attempted to
preserve and transmit it.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

The distinction matters because schools tend to annex the past in retrospect.
Once generations have learnt Potions, Charms, Ancient Runes or Herbology under
those names, the subjects acquire the appearance of timeless divisions. They
were not. A useful plant does not wait for a Herbology lesson before revealing
its virtues, and a rune may carry magic long before it appears on a timetable.
The school subject is an arrangement of knowledge, not proof of the knowledge's
beginning.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002, ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002, ext-a39-001, ext-a42-001 -->

The surviving material does not reveal a general system of education before
Hogwarts. No earlier British school, common curriculum or ordinary course of
childhood instruction can presently be described. That limitation need be
stated only once. It forbids an invented procession of village masters and
family academies; it does not require us to imagine that each generation
rediscovered magic afresh. A society capable of producing the founders had
plainly learnt how to carry difficult knowledge across time, even if the
routes by which it travelled have mostly disappeared.

### Old Arts

Among the oldest things at Hogwarts is an object that was old before the
school existed. The Hogwarts Pensieve is carved from stone and marked with
modified Saxon runes. Its workmanship identifies it as an artefact of immense
antiquity, predating the castle that now contains it. Here, unusually, the
argument does not depend upon reasoning backwards from a later school subject.
Runic magical inscription survives on a pre-Hogwarts object.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002 -->

The Pensieve acquired a story equal to its age. According to one tradition,
the founders found it half-buried on the very spot where they chose to build.
The source calls this legend unsubstantiated, and it should remain so. The
object's age does not prove the tale of its discovery. Indeed, the contrast is
more revealing than certainty would be: a genuinely ancient artefact entered
the memory of a younger institution, and the institution answered the gap in
its knowledge with a founders' legend.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002, ext-a17-003 -->

Not everything ancient at Hogwarts began with Hogwarts. The Pensieve makes
that truth almost embarrassingly solid. Stone had been selected and carved;
runes had been modified and applied to magical purpose; the finished object
had survived long enough to become part of the school. None of this establishes
a British rune school, a pre-Hogwarts curriculum or even the identity of the
maker. It establishes something simpler and firmer: runic craft belonged to
the magical world from which Hogwarts emerged.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002 -->

Another old craft comes to us by way of a less dignified object. A European
manuscript records witches and wizards using flying broomsticks in 962. The
early broom offered one forward speed and the ability to rise, descend and
stop. It was uncomfortable, limited and unlikely to inspire nostalgia in
anyone required to travel far upon it. Its roughness is precisely what makes
it valuable. It shows an everyday magical technology in use and still being
improved in the broad age from which Hogwarts emerged. The uncertain date of
the school's foundation prevents us from saying whether the record falls just
before or just after its opening.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002 -->

The broom also joined travel to discretion. Unlike a conspicuous flying beast,
it could be hidden without much difficulty, and this advantage was recognised
long before secrecy became an international body of law. Timber had to be
chosen, worked and charmed; controls had to answer reliably enough for a rider
to trust them. The manuscript does not name the makers or their teachers, but a
repeatable device already in use is evidence of knowledge passing between
hands. Solitary inspiration may produce a marvel. A working craft requires
memory.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002 -->

Wandcraft carries a longer tradition, though not so tidy a chronology. The
Ollivander shop sign claims a family wandmaking history from 382 BC. A fuller
family account goes further: the present Mr Ollivander believes that his
ancestors reached Britain with the Romans and sold crafted wands to British
wizards whose own instruments were crude and unreliable. The distinction
between the two statements matters. The shop's date is a public tradition, and
the Roman story is a descendant's belief; neither is an unbroken ledger from
antiquity. Both nevertheless preserve the memory of wandmaking as a craft with
a history outside Hogwarts.

<!-- evidence: ps-ch05-007, ext-a41-001 -->

The craft becomes firmer in the medieval record. Geraint Ollivander is named as
a celebrated medieval wandmaker whose written observations survived in later
family practice. Wand wood itself demanded knowledge no classroom label can
contain. Only a minority of trees yield suitable timber, and recognising them
requires long experience; even the presence of Bowtruckles may guide the
trained eye towards a promising tree. Plant knowledge, creature knowledge and
the maker's judgement meet in a single wand before the wand performs any magic
at all.

<!-- evidence: ext-a42-001, ext-a42-002 -->

Later custom also reminds us that crafts do not pass unchanged merely because
their names endure. Before Garrick Ollivander altered his family's practice,
customers often supplied cores that had personal or family importance. His
insistence upon selecting powerful cores, matching them with suitable woods and
allowing the wand to choose its owner met resistance before its results won
admiration. This reform belongs to a much later wandmaker, not to the founders'
century. Its value here is the change itself. What seems an immemorial custom
may be a successful innovation that has simply had time to become familiar.

<!-- evidence: ext-a41-002, ext-a41-003 -->

Creature knowledge survives differently. Dragons, griffins, unicorns,
phoenixes and centaurs entered medieval Muggle art and writing, often in forms
that would have dismayed a competent magizoologist. Their appearance does not
establish a medieval course in the care of magical creatures. It records
encounters, reports and memories strong enough to cross from magical nature
into non-magical culture. A creature need not be correctly classified to
become part of the knowledge of an age.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002 -->

These remains do not add up to an early timetable. They show something more
interesting: a culture of use. People carved runes, worked wood and magical
materials, flew, carried wands, improved tools and learnt the habits of
dangerous creatures. Hogwarts would eventually arrange many kinds of knowledge
under one roof. It did not create the world on which that roof was raised.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002, qtta-ch01-002, ps-ch05-007, ext-a41-001, ext-a42-001, ext-a42-002, fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002 -->

### Knowledge in Use

Craft implies teaching in the broadest sense, although it does not prove a
profession of teachers. A maker must learn why one piece of wood answers and
another fails. A rider must learn how to rise and, rather more urgently, how to
stop. Written observations such as Geraint Ollivander's could preserve part of
a craft; tools and failed attempts preserved other parts. Knowledge might pass
through a family, a workshop, a neighbour or a temporary collaboration. The
record does not choose one route, and there is no reason ordinary life should
have supplied only one.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002, ext-a42-001, ext-a42-002 -->

Learning of this kind need not separate itself neatly from work. Selecting
timber, tending a garden, preparing a remedy or assisting an experienced hand
can all create occasions for instruction without producing a school register.
An old object may preserve decisions its maker never wrote down; an
unsuccessful broom may teach balance in a particularly memorable fashion. Such
means were uneven and local, but they allowed skill to accumulate outside an
institution.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002, ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002, ext-a42-001, ext-a42-002 -->

Potioneering provides a useful test of the difference between a craft and its
later place in the curriculum. Potion-making is genuinely magical. It requires
wandwork, not merely ingredients, heat and obedience to a recipe, and its
effects may be difficult for anyone but another skilled potioneer to undo.
Some potions reproduce work that spells or charms can perform, while others
achieve effects unavailable by those means. Potions are therefore not cooking
with more alarming contents. They are a demanding branch of practical magic.

<!-- evidence: ext-a39-001, ext-a39-002, ext-a39-003 -->

This definition does not date the craft. Direct evidence for potion-making in
tenth-century Britain remains elusive. The medieval record does, however,
allow us to see what happened outside Hogwarts after the school existed. In
the twelfth century, Linfred of Stinchcombe cultivated unusual plants,
experimented behind closed doors and supplied medicinal help to Muggle
neighbours, who took him for an eccentric but useful gardener. His household
was at once garden, place of experiment and local source of healing.

<!-- evidence: ext-a38-001 -->

Wizarding historians credit Linfred with remedies that developed into potions
still known in later centuries, including the ancestors of Skele-gro and
Pepperup Potion. He also sold his cures to other witches and wizards. The
details matter because they show knowledge moving in several directions: from
plants to experiment, from experiment to remedy, from a private household to
neighbours and customers, and from one practitioner's work into wider magical
use.

<!-- evidence: ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002 -->

Linfred's work also resists a tidy division between household knowledge and
specialist magic. His garden and home were local settings, but remedies that
entered wider use were not merely family curiosities. Other witches and wizards
bought them, tested their usefulness in practice and carried them beyond
Stinchcombe. The surviving account does not tell us whether Linfred wrote
instructions, demonstrated his methods or guarded them closely. It does show
that valuable knowledge could move outwards from one household without first
passing through a classroom.

<!-- evidence: ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002 -->

Linfred must not be pushed backwards into the founders' century. He lived in
twelfth-century Britain, when Hogwarts was already established. That later date
is the point. The castle had not swallowed every useful plant, healing practice
or new preparation within its walls. A wizard could work at home, serve a local
community, experiment and contribute lasting remedies to magical practice.
Hogwarts organised knowledge, but it did not acquire a monopoly over it.

<!-- evidence: ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002 -->

The same example loosens the hold of the modern timetable upon the past.
Linfred's plants can be historically important without being called Herbology;
his remedies can contribute to potioneering without beginning as lessons in
Potions. Wandmakers likewise needed knowledge of trees, magical creatures and
inherited materials without dividing their work among three school departments.
The later subjects are useful arrangements. The practices beneath them crossed
their borders with very little concern for administrative tidiness.

<!-- evidence: ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002, ext-a41-002, ext-a42-001 -->

Stories carried another kind of knowledge. In later centuries, tales associated
with Beedle became familiar bedtime reading, and one well-known story taught
many wizarding children that magic could not reverse death. No spell was learnt
from that lesson, yet a limit upon magical action was. Beedle cannot be placed
in the homes of the founders' first pupils, but his later reception shows how a
magical culture may pass on judgement and restraint as well as technique.

<!-- evidence: beedle-fm-002, beedle-ch04-001 -->

The weakness of such scattered teaching is easy to see. Knowledge held by one
family, craft or gifted healer could travel only as far as its keepers and
circumstances allowed. A child born near accomplished practitioners stood in a
different position from one whose magic appeared where little help was at hand.
The record gives no measure of this inequality, but the founders' decision to
seek children rather than wait for them is intelligible against it. Recruitment
made instruction less dependent upon the accident of immediate acquaintance.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Hogwarts thus arose from a world in which magical knowledge was real,
consequential and dispersed. Some of it belonged to stone or tools; some to
skilled hands, gardens and written craft lore; some to stories and cautions.
The school's achievement was not to invent all this knowledge, but to create a
place in which much of it could be taught repeatedly. Before considering that
change, however, the landscape in which those hands and households worked must
be restored. Magical Britain did not occupy a separate island.

### Among Muggles

Magical and non-magical Britain shared roads, fields, forests, harbours and
settlements. Before the International Statute of Secrecy, they also shared
ordinary ways of getting about. Witches, wizards and Muggles travelled in
horse-drawn carts and sailing ships; in defence, a wizard might use a sword as
well as a wand. These accounts cover a broad pre-Statute past rather than the
founders' particular decade, but they place magical people where they belong:
inside historical Britain, not beside it in a sealed and parallel country.

<!-- evidence: ext-a08-003, ext-a45-001 -->

Shared transport placed magical travellers within many of the same practical
journeys as everyone else. A cart followed a road; a sailing ship depended upon
harbours, crews and weather, however much magic might ease one part of the
passage. The record does not tell us how openly every traveller declared
themselves, nor what enchantments they used along the way. Its importance is
more ordinary. Magical people did not need a wholly separate system for every
task. They could adopt the same useful thing as their neighbours and remain
magical while doing so.

<!-- evidence: ext-a45-001 -->

Even the cauldron once belonged to both populations. Muggles and magical people
used the same suspended cooking pots before changing household technologies
left the cauldron chiefly in wizarding hands, where it remained suited to
potion-making over a naked flame. The source supplies no century for this
change, so it cannot furnish the founders' kitchen. It does preserve a small
but telling history: an ordinary object could be shared, adapted and eventually
come to look peculiarly magical.

<!-- evidence: ext-a40-001, ext-a40-002 -->

The broom belongs to the same mixed landscape. Its rider used magic, but the
object itself was commonplace enough to hide. The choice reveals a world in
which Muggle observation was already a practical concern, though the two
populations had not yet been divided by the later law of secrecy. Discretion
was exercised through ordinary things: a broom that could be put away, a road
travelled without display, a remote place chosen because repeated magic would
be difficult to explain.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002, cos-ch09-003 -->

Muggle records show how imperfect that discretion could be. Medieval art and
literature remembered magical creatures while confusing their names and
natures. One writer known as Brother Benedict described a violent encounter in
a herb garden with what he took to be a monstrous ferret. Later magical
interpretation identifies a Jarvey, probably hunting gnomes. A cultivated
garden, a magical creature and a bewildered observer occupied the same patch of
ground; the record kept the incident and misunderstood nearly all of it.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002, fb-ch02-003 -->

Such records prove proximity, not easy intimacy. A Muggle might know that
something extraordinary had been seen without knowing what it was, whether a
witch or wizard was nearby, or how the event fitted into a larger magical
world. Magical people possessed names and techniques that their neighbours
often replaced with marvel, monster or mistake. The two groups could use the
same road or vessel while carrying very different explanations of what they
met along the way.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002, fb-ch02-003, ext-a45-001 -->

The relationship was never one simple condition. Shared tools and transport
did not prevent fear; fear did not erase every useful neighbour or ordinary
exchange. Linfred's Muggle neighbours sought his remedies without recognising
their magical origin. Medieval writers recorded creatures they could not
classify. A later law would formalise separation, but before it did so, daily
life allowed knowledge, service, suspicion and error to sit uncomfortably close
together.

<!-- evidence: ext-a38-001, ext-a40-001, ext-a45-001, fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002, fb-ch02-003 -->

This was the setting in which magical learning acquired a problem of place. A
single act of instruction or experiment might pass unnoticed. The repeated
gathering of magical children, teachers, equipment and practice was harder to
hide. The more successfully a centre collected knowledge, the more traffic and
unusual activity it produced. Continuity needed somewhere to live, but a place
made continuity visible.

<!-- evidence: ext-a38-001, qtta-ch01-002, ext-a40-001, ext-a45-001, cos-ch09-003 -->

### Fear and Distance

The strongest founding account places Muggle fear and the persecution of
witches and wizards beside the choice of a remote site. The founders built away
from prying eyes. We are given no map of attacks and no exact year in which the
danger peaked. What the account makes plain is that concealment belonged to the
castle's purpose; it was not a picturesque quality supplied by later legend.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Medieval creature history helps explain how fear could spread. Reports of
dragons and Hippogriffs entered communities poorly equipped to distinguish one
magical danger from another. Misunderstanding did not remain safely between the
covers of a bestiary. A frightening encounter might enlarge suspicion of magic
and of the people believed to practise it. The dates are broad, but the pattern
is clear enough: incomplete knowledge could make the extraordinary more
alarming, not less.

<!-- evidence: fb-ch02-003, fb-ch02-004 -->

Coexistence and persecution were not two tidy ages separated by a single
change of mind. Contact created occasions for recognition, curiosity,
misunderstanding and fear, and the response could differ from one place to the
next. Linfred's neighbours could value an eccentric healer in the twelfth
century even though hostility towards magic existed elsewhere and at other
times. No catalogue of invented raids is needed. Magical practice was visible
enough to attract danger, and a permanent concentration of it demanded more
than the discretion of an individual household.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, fb-ch02-004, ext-a38-001 -->

Persecution alone does not explain why four practitioners chose to become
educators, or why their answer took the form of a school. It does explain why
their school could not safely be merely a large house in a busy settlement.
Distance reduced casual observation, gave room for repeated practice and
allowed magical children to gather without every lesson becoming a public
demonstration. The castle's situation joined educational ambition to the
practical requirements of survival.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

The distinction between secrecy and isolation is important. Hogwarts had to
remain connected to the society it served. Pupils were sought and brought to
it; people, messages and supplies necessarily crossed the distance between the
castle and the communities beyond. Remoteness controlled access rather than
ending it. A hidden school could reach out because its centre was protected.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Safety was not the school's sole importance. A refuge may preserve people for
a season; a school must preserve knowledge through repetition. The remote site
supplied time as well as distance: time for instruction to recur, for errors to
be corrected, for several teachers to compare practice, and for one group of
pupils to be followed by another. The castle made continuity material.

### A Place for Learning

At the threshold of Hogwarts, scattered features become an institution. There
is a fixed place, more than one accomplished teacher, an intention to seek
young people showing magic, and a continuing purpose of education. The
founders did not invent magical childhood, skilled practice, runes, wands or
the need for discretion. Their deliberate combination of place, teachers and
pupils was new in consequence, whether or not it was unique in form.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, ext-a17-002, ps-ch05-007, ext-a41-001 -->

The castle gave knowledge an address. A craft carried in memory may disappear
when its keeper dies or withholds it; a practice attached to one household may
remain local. A school can assemble several practices, repeat instruction and
retain habits beyond the lives of particular teachers. It can also bring into
conversation methods that had developed apart. Hogwarts did not instantly
produce the mature curriculum of later centuries, but it created the setting
in which magical education could accumulate a history of its own.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003, ext-a17-002, qtta-ch01-002, ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002, ext-a42-002 -->

Recruitment was equally consequential. By seeking children who displayed
magic, the founders made instruction an undertaking that reached beyond their
immediate company. The record does not define the first catchment or promise
that every magical child was found. Its significance is more practical. A
child's access to teaching no longer depended entirely upon appearing at the
right door; the new institution accepted some responsibility for finding the
pupil.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Bringing pupils together changed the possibilities of learning. A child could
observe not only an experienced adult but other learners: their errors,
aptitudes and different inheritances of knowledge. Teachers could repeat a
lesson, discover where explanation failed and distinguish an individual
difficulty from a common one. These are ordinary consequences of collective
education, not a recovered timetable from the first year. They help explain
why a permanent school could become more than a sheltered collection of
private lessons.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

The institution also made disagreement durable. In an informal arrangement,
teachers who differed could simply part; in a shared school, their differences
had to be negotiated, expressed in practice or allowed to threaten the common
enterprise. Hogwarts would eventually acquire offices, customs and divisions
through which authority was exercised. None need be imagined complete at the
beginning. The decisive change was that several powerful practitioners had
bound their educational purposes to one place and to successive groups of
children.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Permanence is a judgement supplied by what followed. The founders could not
have known every danger their school would survive, and a castle alone does not
guarantee an institution's future. Yet a durable site, active recruitment and
repeated instruction made survival beyond the founders possible. Once pupils
became former pupils, the school could extend through people as well as stone:
its knowledge, loyalties and disputes returned with them to the wider magical
community.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

Nothing in these conditions made Hogwarts inevitable. Broom flight did not
require a school; Linfred's remedies flourished outside one; Muggle fear did
not require four particular founders; magical children did not require a
castle. The institution was one answer to a world of dispersed knowledge,
uneven access and dangerous visibility. It became historically decisive
because that answer endured.

<!-- evidence: qtta-ch01-002, ext-a38-001, ext-a38-002, cos-ch09-003 -->

Before Hogwarts, then, Britain was not waiting in ignorance. Witches and
wizards already possessed ancient objects, runic craft, tools, skilled trades
and formidable magic. Knowledge moved through use and association, even though
its ordinary paths are poorly recorded. Magical creatures and magical
travellers crossed a landscape shared with Muggles, leaving traces in
manuscripts, art and fear. What the school added was a protected centre: a
place to which children could be brought and in which knowledge could be made
to outlast the circumstances of its keeper.

<!-- evidence: ext-a17-002, qtta-ch01-002, ps-ch05-007, ext-a41-001, ext-a42-001, ext-a42-002, fb-ch02-001, fb-ch02-002, fb-ch02-003, fb-ch02-004, ext-a45-001, cos-ch09-003 -->

Only at this point do the four founders properly enter the history. Their
later fame makes their cooperation appear natural and their divisions
foreseeable. Neither impression should be trusted without examination. The
world that made their school significant can now be seen in outline; the next
question is who they were, what purposes they brought to the undertaking, and
how much of their familiar character belongs to evidence rather than to the
long memory of Hogwarts.
