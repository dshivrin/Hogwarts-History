# Chapter Three

## The Founding of Hogwarts

### An Idea Becomes a Place

The four founders had agreed upon more than the desirability of teaching. They meant to gather young magical people into one school and to keep that school in being. The distinction is easily missed. A gifted witch or wizard might teach a handful of pupils for a season; a school required a place, a community and arrangements that would still function when its first teachers no longer could.

Hogwarts was founded more than a thousand years ago. Its precise date was already uncertain in the most careful surviving account, and neither year nor anniversary can responsibly be supplied. What can be recovered is not a ceremonial beginning but a sequence of practical acts. The founders chose a place apart, built a castle there, sought children who showed signs of magic, brought them to the school and educated them together.

The achievement lay in the combination. Britain already had magical children, accomplished practitioners and knowledge worth passing on. Hogwarts joined those elements in a shared institution capable of continuing.

### A Place Apart

Hogwarts stands in Scotland, although no trustworthy account narrows its position to a valley, mountain or shore that could be marked for the curious. Its remoteness was deliberate. The founders built far from prying Muggle eyes in an age when magic was feared and witches and wizards suffered persecution.

That explanation is strong enough without improvement. The record does not name an attack that drove the founders into the north, nor does it describe a search for defensible heights, useful plants or a magically powerful tract of ground. Distance itself answered the stated danger. A lesson given in a household might pass unnoticed; a permanent gathering of magical children, teachers and equipment was much harder to conceal in an ordinary settlement.

Remoteness did not mean withdrawal from magical society. The school existed to receive pupils, and the founders went out to find them. The chosen place therefore had to be secluded without becoming useless. How that balance was managed—by which roads, escorts or forms of magical travel—the earliest accounts do not say. What they establish is a protected centre connected to a population beyond its walls.

### Raising Hogwarts

The founders built the castle together. This is a statement of shared responsibility, not a surviving works account. No architect is named. No builders' roll, sequence of construction or collection of foundation spells has come down to us. We cannot say how long the work took, whether every founder shaped every part of it, or how much of the present castle belonged to the first design.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

The silence is considerable, but it does not make the event small. The founders did not choose a cottage in which four masters might receive visitors. They established a castle capable of sheltering teaching and pupils through more than a single term or generation. Its scale declared permanence before any later rule or custom did.

One later account speaks of the castle's completion, but completion need not mean immobility. A school may be ready to receive its community and still alter for centuries afterwards. Hogwarts has accumulated too much history for every stair, tower and chamber to be pressed back into its first days. The foundation gives us the castle as an institution; the changing fabric of the building has a history of its own.

### A School Hidden from the World

The site's distance was the first protection that can be connected securely with the founding. Hogwarts was placed beyond ordinary Muggle notice because discovery carried danger. Whether the founders also laid the particular concealments familiar in later centuries is not recorded.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-003 -->

A Muggle approaching Hogwarts does not see the inhabited castle. The enchantment presents a mouldering ruin and a warning that entry is unsafe. It is a neat defence because it turns curiosity back upon itself: the intruder is not confronted with an impossible sight but supplied with a thoroughly ordinary reason to leave. Yet the age of this enchantment is unknown. It demonstrates that concealment remained part of Hogwarts's protection, not that its precise form was fixed on the day the school opened.

<!-- evidence: gof-ch11-005 -->

The distinction matters. Hogwarts has also been protected in later times against forms of magical movement and intrusion, but their existence does not make them founders' work. Even the familiar claim that a secret magical place may be made impossible to plot on a map does not, in the surviving passage, establish that Hogwarts itself was so treated. The foundation can safely be credited with the purpose of concealment; the full architecture of protection belongs to the castle's longer history.

### The First School

Once the castle existed, the founders sought out young people who showed signs of magic and brought them there to be educated. This is the clearest picture of Hogwarts at work in its first years: four founders, pupils gathered from beyond the site, and teaching carried on in common. They continued in harmony for several years.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004 -->

The account does not permit us to furnish their first classroom. It gives no age for the youngest pupil, no number for the first company, no list of lessons and no proof that the later seven-year course already existed. Nor does it tell us whether all pupils arrived together or whether the school grew as children were found. A medieval school should not be dressed in the timetable and offices of a much later Hogwarts merely because the same walls eventually contained them.

What makes the early gathering a school is not a reconstructed schedule but repetition. Children were sought, brought and taught; the work continued for years. The founders were not entertaining occasional apprentices. They had created a place in which one group of pupils could be followed by another.

The school also required labour beyond the act of teaching. A later record names Hankerton Humble as a caretaker appointed by all four founders. It does not call him the first caretaker or reveal the extent of his duties, and one name cannot conjure an entire original staff. It does show that the founders' common work had begun to produce offices and responsibilities belonging to Hogwarts rather than to any one founder.

### Built to Outlast Its Builders

The founders still faced a difficulty created by their own success. A school that depended upon four people to discover every magical child could not easily survive them. The strongest later account of their answer places two objects in a locked tower when the castle was completed: the Quill of Acceptance and the Book of Admittance.

<!-- evidence: ext-a03-001, ext-a03-002 -->

<!-- source-access: ext-a03-001 through ext-a03-004 are official retrospective accounts published after 1984. They clarify a founder-era act and continuing institution; the reconstructed narrative does not adopt their later examples or wording. -->

When a child first gives a sign of magic, the Quill attempts to write the child's name and the Book decides whether the evidence is sufficient. The source calls the pair Hogwarts's sole means of selection. It does not follow that the objects performed the whole work of admission. A name written in a book does not find a household, persuade a family or bring a pupil from home to the castle. The founders' personal search and the instruments' judgement answer different parts of the same problem.

<!-- evidence: cos-ch09-004, ext-a03-002, ext-a03-003, ext-a03-004 -->

The Book and Quill made recognition independent of a founder's chance encounter. Their magic did not provide a curriculum or a teacher, but it allowed the school to know that another magical child existed. In that narrow and essential respect, Hogwarts had acquired a way to continue receiving pupils beyond the reach and lifetime of its makers.

The same problem appears in a more famous tradition. The Sorting Hat's own songs maintain that the founders placed something of their judgement into Godric Gryffindor's hat so that the choosing of pupils could continue after their deaths. The full history of that choice belongs to the four Houses. Here the tradition matters for a simpler reason: the founders are remembered as asking how the school would act when they were gone.

### One School, Four Legacies

Hogwarts altered magical education because its work could be repeated after its founders were gone. The castle supplied a lasting place; concealment protected the gathering; repeated teaching made the place a school; and enduring magical instruments helped it find and choose pupils whom its founders could never meet. None of these arrangements proves that the first Hogwarts possessed every feature of the later institution. Together they show that permanence was built into its purpose.

The founders had made one school, but they had not ceased to be four distinct teachers. Their cooperation gave Hogwarts its life; their different judgements about promise gave that life an internal shape. Those differences did not remain private opinions for long. They became the four Houses through which generations of pupils would enter the school the founders had made.
