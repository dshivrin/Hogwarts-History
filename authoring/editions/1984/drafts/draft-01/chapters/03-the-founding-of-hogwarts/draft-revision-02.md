# The Founding of Hogwarts

The four founders had agreed upon more than the desirability of teaching. They
meant to gather young magical people into one school and to keep that school in
being. The distinction is easily overlooked. A gifted witch or wizard might
teach a handful of pupils for a season; a school required a place, repeated
work and arrangements that would continue when its first teachers no longer
could.

Hogwarts was founded more than a thousand years before the accounts from which
its beginnings are chiefly known. The precise date was already uncertain in
the most careful surviving account, and neither year nor anniversary can
responsibly be supplied. What can be recovered is not a ceremonial opening but
a sequence of practical acts. The founders chose a place apart, built a castle
there, sought children who showed signs of magic, brought them to the school
and educated them together.

The achievement lay in the combination. Magical Britain already possessed
children with ability, accomplished practitioners and knowledge worth passing
on. Hogwarts joined those elements within an institution intended to endure.
It was made remote because a permanent gathering of magical people was both
more useful and more conspicuous than instruction scattered among households
and individual teachers.

Later testimony places Hogwarts in Scotland, though it discloses no exact
valley, mountain or shore. These accounts were recorded long after the
founding and do not reveal how the site was chosen. They establish the country,
not a route by which the curious might approach it or a landscape with which
to decorate the founders' deliberations.

The reason for remoteness is better founded. The founders built away from
Muggle observation in an age when magic was feared and witches and wizards
were persecuted. Wider medieval evidence shows how misunderstood creatures and
magic could nourish fear, but it does not identify an attack that drove the
founders north or a single event that caused the school. Distance itself
answered the danger named by the historical account.

Seclusion did not mean withdrawal from magical society. A school intended to
receive pupils had to remain connected to people beyond its grounds. The place
therefore solved two problems at once: it allowed magical practice to recur
away from ordinary Muggle notice while providing a centre to which children
could be brought. How the founders balanced remoteness with travel is not
recorded. Roads, escorts and magical conveyances belong to the silence, not to
the history.

The founders built the castle together. This is a statement of joint
responsibility, not a surviving works account. No architect is named. No
builders' roll, sequence of construction, land grant or collection of
foundation spells has come down to us. We cannot say how long the work took,
whether each founder shaped every part of it or how much of the present castle
belonged to the first design.

The absence of engineering detail should not make the event appear small. The
founders did not choose a cottage in which four teachers might receive
occasional visitors. They established a castle capable of sheltering pupils
and instruction beyond a single term or generation. Its scale made permanence
material before the institution possessed the offices, habits and ceremonies
later associated with it.

One later official account speaks of the castle's completion. The phrase marks
a point at which the founders could install an enduring part of Hogwarts's
admission process; it does not establish a building thereafter fixed in every
stone. A school may be ready to receive its community and still change for
centuries. The foundation gives us a completed place of operation, not every
later stair, tower and chamber pressed back into its first days.

Remoteness was the first protection that can be connected securely with the
foundation. A familiar concealment existed by the later period for which it is
recorded: a Muggle approaching Hogwarts would see a mouldering ruin and a
warning that entry was unsafe rather than the inhabited castle. The defence is
ingenious because it offers curiosity an ordinary reason to turn back. Its age,
however, is unknown. It demonstrates that concealment remained part of
Hogwarts's protection, not that the founders devised this particular effect.

Once the castle existed, the founders sought young people who displayed signs
of magic, brought them to Hogwarts and educated them. This is the clearest
surviving picture of the school in operation: four founders, pupils gathered
from beyond the site and teaching carried on for several years. The account
does not name the first child, count the first company or record a first
lesson. Nor does it establish that the later seven-year course, timetable or
curriculum existed from the beginning.

The early gathering should not be dressed in the offices of a mature Hogwarts
merely because the same walls eventually contained them. Its institutional
character lies in repetition rather than familiar detail. Children were sought,
brought and taught; the work continued. The founders were not entertaining a
single circle of apprentices. They had created a place in which one gathering
of pupils could be followed by another.

Later evidence supplies one narrow sign of work beyond teaching. Hankerton
Humble is remembered as a caretaker appointed by all four founders. The source
does not call him the first caretaker, describe his duties or establish a
complete founder-era staff. One name cannot conjure an original hierarchy. It
does show a responsibility attached to Hogwarts itself and an appointment made
through the founders' common authority.

The founders also faced a difficulty created by the school's intended reach.
An institution that depended entirely upon four people encountering every
magical child could scarcely survive them. A later official account places two
objects in a locked tower at the castle's completion: the Quill of Acceptance
and the Book of Admittance. When a child first gives a sign of magic, the Quill
attempts to write the name; the Book admits it only when the evidence is
sufficient. Together they are described as Hogwarts's means of selection.

Selection did not perform every act of admission. A name entered in a book did
not by itself find a household, explain the school to a family or carry a pupil
to the castle. Binns's account of the founders seeking children and the later
account of the instruments need not compete if they are kept to what each
establishes. The objects judged the appearance of magic; people still had to
make education possible. Whether personal searching continued after the
installation, and how the two practices were coordinated, is not recorded.

That distinction is a bounded inference rather than a recovered procedure, but
its consequence is substantial. The Book and Quill made recognition less
dependent upon a founder's chance encounter. They did not supply a teacher,
curriculum, invitation or journey. They enabled Hogwarts to know that another
magical child existed and to preserve the judgement by which that child might
be accepted.

The problem of survival appears again in the school's best-known founder
tradition. The Sorting Hat's songs and later accounts maintain that all four
founders placed something of their judgement into Godric Gryffindor's hat so
that the choosing of pupils could continue after their deaths. The story is a
tradition, not a dated technical record. Its full meaning belongs to the
history of the Houses and of the Sorting ceremony. At the foundation it
expresses a simpler concern: the founders were remembered as preparing the
school to act when they themselves were gone.

The Book and Quill and the Hat did not answer the same question. One tradition
concerns recognising sufficient magic for admission; the other concerns the
assignment of accepted pupils according to founder judgements. Treating them
as a single mechanism would erase the distinction between entering Hogwarts
and entering one of its Houses. Their shared significance is continuity: both
place an institutional decision beyond the founders' immediate presence.

Hogwarts had therefore become more than a castle and more than four teachers.
Its location protected a gathering; repeated teaching made that gathering a
school; a caretaker represented one limited responsibility belonging to the
institution; and enduring magical objects helped it recognise and choose
pupils whom its builders would never meet. None of this proves that the first
Hogwarts possessed every feature of the later school. It shows that
continuation was built into its work.

The founders had made one institution, but they had not ceased to be four
distinct educators. Their cooperation gave Hogwarts life, while their
different judgements about promise gave that life an internal shape. Those
judgements need not be repeated here as four portraits. Their next historical
consequence was structural: the common school preserved its founders'
differences by giving them the durable form of Houses.
