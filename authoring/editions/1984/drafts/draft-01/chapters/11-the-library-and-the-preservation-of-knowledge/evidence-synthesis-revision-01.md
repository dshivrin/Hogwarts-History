## Chapter purpose

The chapter begins where Chapter 10 ends: with the written store to which
pupils turned when memory, rumour and ordinary experience failed. It explains
how required books, library holdings, older works and records carried knowledge
beyond any one lesson or teacher; how pupils used that store for work and
independent inquiry; and how permissions, removal and collection disputes
limited access. It ends by distinguishing written preservation from the living
and represented memory reserved for Chapter 12.

## Executive finding

The proposed argument is supported, but the chronology is uneven. Pre-1984
evidence confirms older school records, Riddle's research, long-approved
textbook use, old cultural texts and a recognised boundary around extreme Dark
magic. The detailed working life of the library—study tables, subject shelves,
circulation, Pince's supervision and the teacher-note procedure—is documented
chiefly in the 1990s and must remain later evidence.

Within that boundary, Hogwarts clearly maintained more than course books.
Later pupils consulted reference works, legal precedents, specialist creature
books, newspapers, historical material and popular sporting history. Research
could succeed only after prolonged searching or fail altogether. The
Restricted Section preserved advanced or dangerous works while limiting pupil
access; later, Dumbledore removed more explicit Horcrux books without
destroying them. These are distinct acts and do not establish one complete
restriction policy.

The school also retained records outside the ordinary pupil collection.
Disciplinary files, prefect lists, trophies, award material and the much-later
Hogwarts Archives represent different forms of administrative memory and
different access conditions. The post-1984 Malfoy challenge to *Beedle* then
shows the library becoming a contested institutional space: a governor sought
removal, the headmaster refused, and a Board majority supported continued
access. The evidence therefore supports a bounded tension between preservation
and regulation, not a claim that the library was timeless, comprehensive or
open without limit.
