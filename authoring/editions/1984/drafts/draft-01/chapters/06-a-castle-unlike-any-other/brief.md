## Chapter purpose

Explain what Hogwarts Castle became after the founding era: not a fixed shell
containing school history, but a magical environment whose routes, thresholds,
rooms, uses, and concealed layers accumulated across centuries.

## Chapter argument

Hogwarts cannot be described like an ordinary historic building with a stable
floor plan. Its most public room can reproduce the sky; its stairs, steps, and
doors make navigation conditional; some thresholds enforce institutional rules;
and some rooms respond to need, withhold themselves from an imprecise seeker,
or escape even sophisticated magical mapping. These behaviours do not prove
that every feature dates from the founders. The strongest dated alteration—the
eighteenth-century preservation of a much older Chamber entrance beneath new
plumbing—shows instead that Hogwarts accumulated layers. Later inhabitants
could refit rooms, cover older access points, and hide new uses inside inherited
stone. The castle is therefore a historical source of an unusual kind: it
preserves old work, later adaptation, collective habit, and forgotten intention
without arranging them into a single legible plan.

## Entry and exit

- **Entry point:** Chapter 5 ends with a castle that survived its makers and
  preserved a founder-era secret through later alteration. Chapter 6 begins by
  widening that single example into the castle's general architectural problem.
- **Exit point:** the interior has been shown as only one part of a larger
  magical place; the narrative can widen naturally beyond walls and towers
  towards the surrounding estate without announcing Chapter 7.

## Included subjects

- the Great Hall's enchanted ceiling, with serious weight given to its explicit
  association with *Hogwarts: A History*;
- stairs whose destination or footing cannot always be treated as fixed;
- doors that require manners or touch rather than ordinary handles, and doors
  disguised as walls;
- concealed shortcuts and the practical local knowledge required to navigate;
- architecture that responds to a user's identity or to an inherited rule;
- the Room of Requirement as a need-responsive, conditionally accessible,
  differently configured, and imperfectly mapped space;
- long accumulation within hidden rooms;
- the distinction between a magical map of Hogwarts and a complete fixed plan;
- later refitting of existing rooms;
- the eighteenth-century adaptation that preserved an older Chamber entrance;
- the castle as accumulated historical evidence rather than a catalogue of
  curiosities.

## Excluded or deferred subjects

- retelling the founding, House histories, Slytherin's departure, Chamber
  legend, Basilisk, or Chamber rediscovery;
- the wider grounds, lake, Forest, gates, approaches, Hogsmeade geography, and
  external boundaries;
- a catalogue of common rooms, dormitories, classrooms, towers, dungeons,
  bathrooms, offices, portraits, ghosts, secret passages, or defensive spells;
- the nature and society of portraits or ghosts;
- governance, curriculum, ordinary pupil life, library history, ceremonies,
  Quidditch, school crises, headmaster biographies, or modern wars;
- claims about a complete founder-era plan, builders, renovation programme,
  dates of undated enchantments, or the origin of the Room of Requirement;
- treating later discovery as ordinary knowledge available to the reconstructed
  1984 narrator.

## Presentation model

Use the unified narrative model. The reconstructed Bagshot narrative may state
the Great Hall fact explicitly attributed to *Hogwarts: A History*, describe
well-established pre-1984 architecture, and make bounded historical
interpretations. Harry-era observations, post-1984 uses of the Room of
Requirement, official retrospective accounts, and author-interview commentary
must be identifiable later editorial evidence. External interview transcripts
are authority-D preservation carriers and may support an editorial conclusion
about the inadequacy of a fixed floor plan, but they must not be voiced as an
in-universe historical source.
