## Chapter purpose

Reconstruct Hogwarts as a residential community: the rhythms, spaces, habits,
freedoms, inconveniences, relationships, obligations and traditions that
filled a pupil's time between formal lessons. The chapter asks not merely
where pupils slept or ate, but how the school structured a day and how pupils
made a life within that structure.

## Central historical question

What was ordinary life like for a Hogwarts pupil, and how did institutional
discipline, communal residence and pupil independence coexist?

## Research argument

The evidence supports a qualified form of the proposed argument. Later
observations show a day anchored by House residence, communal meals, lessons,
homework and after-hours limits, while leaving substantial intervals in which
pupils navigated the castle, studied, socialised and organised recreation with
limited direct adult supervision. Houses made a large school socially legible;
the Great Hall repeatedly assembled it; prefects extended supervision through
student hierarchy; and older pupils acquired both wider privileges and heavier
responsibilities. Hogwarts did not leave pupils wholly unmanaged, but much of
its management was intermittent and layered rather than continuous.

The strongest pre-1984 evidence confirms particular practices and social
conditions rather than a complete day: Dippet-era Christmas festivities,
Riddle-era financial assistance and student networking, Marauders-era
friendship, rivalry and bullying, and an earlier detention imposed on
Gilderoy Lockhart. Harry-era evidence supplies most of the detailed routine
and must remain visibly later.

## Entry and exit

- **Entry:** fulfil Chapter 9's closing promise by moving from educational
  structure to the experience of waking, eating, moving, working and
  socialising inside it.
- **Exit:** establish that the castle became school, home and social world at
  once, then move towards Chapter 11 without attempting a history of the
  library or its restrictions.

## Required subjects

- the rhythm rather than a fabricated hourly timetable: waking, meals,
  lesson periods, homework, free time, evening and after-hours boundaries;
- House common rooms and dormitories as residential and social communities;
- the Great Hall as ordinary dining room, post office, notice venue and public
  school assembly;
- homework, library and common-room study, peer assistance, procrastination,
  revision and later-year workload;
- informal recreation, House sport as social culture, grounds use and bounded
  evidence for clubs or games;
- friendship, rivalry, bullying, romance, reputation, blood-status prejudice
  and pupil economic difference without turning the chapter into biography;
- owl post, letters, parcels, Howlers and outside news;
- weekends, Hogsmeade permissions and the age-related expansion of freedom;
- Christmas residence and celebration, including the Dippet-era pantomime,
  plus Easter and summer boundaries where supported;
- rules, prefects, detentions, point loss and exceptional abusive punishment
  as experienced by pupils;
- castle navigation, ghosts, Peeves, health care, pets, trunks, robes and
  other material details only when they shape lived experience.

## Explicit exclusions

- a universal minute-by-minute timetable or invented bell schedule;
- another curriculum, examinations or teaching-method chapter;
- another architectural tour of common rooms, the Great Hall or the castle;
- formal governance analysis already owned by Chapter 8;
- a rulebook catalogue or a history of institutional punishments;
- a Quidditch history, Hogsmeade guide or library history;
- biographies of individual pupils or a summary of Harry Potter's school
  years;
- generalisation from isolated hobbies, exceptional crises or Ministry-era
  abuses;
- silent projection of 1991–1997 practice into 1984.

## Presentation model

Use the unified narrative model. Build a representative rhythm from multiple
dated observations without inventing a representative pupil or implying that
one documented day governed every cohort. Pre-1984 memories and later
descriptions of earlier practice enter through explicit temporal pivots.
Harry-era routines remain later evidence, with adjacent source-access comments
stating what they do and do not establish.
