## Executive finding

The proposed argument is supported if “independence” is kept relative rather
than absolute. Later evidence shows a school day with fixed anchors—House
residence, meals, lessons, assignments and after-hours limits—but also shows
pupils moving between those anchors with considerable responsibility for
finding rooms, completing work, choosing company and organising recreation.
Supervision was distributed among teachers, Heads of House, prefects, the
caretaker, portrait gatekeepers, ghosts, rules, House points and peer pressure.
It was therefore intermittent and layered, not absent.

The House functioned as home, social centre and first level of student
authority. The Great Hall repeatedly made the whole school visible through
meals, post, announcements, outside news and public House competition.
Academic work extended into common rooms, libraries, weekends and holidays;
older pupils gained privileges but also heavier work and supervisory duties.
Hogsmeade marked a documented later expansion of freedom from third year,
subject to guardian permission. Communication and holiday residence kept home
and school connected rather than wholly separate.

Pre-1984 evidence is fragmentary but important. It establishes financial aid
for an unsupported entrant, influential student circles, Marauders-era
friendship and bullying, an earlier detention, and Dippet-era Christmas
festivity. These points prevent the chapter from treating Harry's cohort as
the beginning of residential school culture, while still falling far short of
a complete 1984 timetable.
