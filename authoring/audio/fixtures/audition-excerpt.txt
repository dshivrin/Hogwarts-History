The history of Hogwarts begins before Hogwarts had a history of its own. A school preserves registers, rules, appointments, disputes and recollections; the world preceding it leaves no such convenient deposit. What survives from that world is scattered among later histories, old objects, specialist books and the imperfect notice of Muggles. The fragments are unequal, but they are not silent. They describe a magical society already in possession of skilled arts, already sharing Britain with its non-magical neighbours, and already facing the difficulty of remaining visible without becoming vulnerable.

Hogwarts is older than the present millennium. Its precise year of foundation was uncertain even in the account most commonly taught at the school, and no day or season can responsibly be supplied. The secure outline is spare. Four witches and wizards built a castle far from Muggle observation, sought out young people who showed signs of magic, brought them to that place and educated them there. The founders, the gathering of pupils, the remoteness of the site and the purpose of instruction belong to history; much of the picturesque detail later attached to them belongs elsewhere.

That outline is enough to correct one persistent illusion. Hogwarts did not bring magic into an empty country. The people who made the school were already accomplished practitioners, and the children they sought displayed magic before the school received them. Knowledge, skill and magical identity therefore existed on both sides of the foundation. What changed was not the existence of magic but the manner in which part of magical society attempted to preserve and transmit it.

The distinction matters because schools tend to annex the past in retrospect. Once generations have learnt Potions, Charms or Herbology under those names, the subjects acquire the appearance of timeless divisions. Earlier practice need not have been so neatly arranged. The ability to recognise a useful plant may be older than a discipline devoted to plants; a healing preparation may be older than the classroom in which its ingredients are later memorised. The school subject is an arrangement of knowledge, not proof of the knowledge's beginning.

The surviving material does not reveal a general system of education before Hogwarts. No earlier British school, common curriculum or ordinary course of childhood instruction can presently be described. That limitation should be stated once and then allowed to do its work. It forbids an invented procession of village masters and family academies; it does not require us to imagine that each generation rediscovered magic afresh. A society capable of producing the founders had plainly learnt how to carry difficult knowledge across time, even if the routes by which it travelled have mostly disappeared.
